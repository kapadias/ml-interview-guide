01

Retrieval training

Highest value

He will poke here because it is the half of Search & Discovery that is hardest to fake. Everything in this part reduces to one sentence: a two-tower model learns exactly what your negatives teach it, and nothing else. The architecture is the easy part.

Two towers, and why they never meet until serving

Offer depth

"The interesting consequence is that the item tower can't see the query, so anything query-conditional has to be pushed into the embedding space geometry. That's why negative mining matters more here than in ranking — want me to go into it?"

Negatives: the actual curriculum

The failure everyone has seen: train on in-batch negatives alone, get a model that matches keywords without matching intent. It nails "boots" and cheerfully returns adult boots for "toddler boots," because no training example ever forced it to separate them.

The three things to say about hard negatives, in order

1. Where from. Mine with the current model against the full index (ANCE-style), refreshed every epoch or two — negatives mined once against the initial model go stale and stop being hard. 2. Which rank band. Not top-10 — take ranks ~20–200. The top of the list is where false negatives live. 3. How many. Too many and training destabilises: the model collapses toward separating near-duplicates and loses coarse structure. One to four per positive, plus full in-batch, is the working range.

Offer depth — denoising

"The clean fix for false negatives is to score mined candidates with the cross-encoder teacher and drop anything above a threshold. You're already paying for that teacher if you're distilling, so it's nearly free."

Sampled softmax and the logQ correction

The full softmax denominator runs over 10M items. Sampled softmax replaces it with a sample — but negatives sampled from the click stream appear in proportion to their popularity. The model therefore learns to push popular items down, because it sees them as negatives far more often than chance. That is a bias you introduced, not a property of the data.

The correction, said precisely

Score used in the loss: s'(q,i) = s(q,i) − log Q(i), where Q(i) is the probability item i is drawn as a negative. In practice Q is estimated by streaming frequency with a decaying count-min sketch, not recomputed in batch. Applied at training only — removed at serving. Leaving it in at serving is a real and common bug; it shows up as popular items mysteriously under-retrieved.

Offer depth

"There's a judgement call underneath it: logQ corrects the sampling bias, but popularity is also genuine signal in commerce. Fully correcting can hurt engagement metrics. I'd treat the correction strength as a tunable and let the A/B decide, rather than assuming the unbiased estimator is the business-optimal one."

Cross-encoder → bi-encoder distillation

Commit

"Distillation is the highest-leverage thing you can do to a two-tower model, because it converts a one-positive label into a fully-scored candidate list — it fixes the supervision, not the architecture."

  • Teacher. Cross-encoder over (query, title + attributes), trained on human labels plus engagement. It can attend across the pair, so it knows "5T" and "toddler size 5" are the same constraint.
  • Transfer. Score the teacher over ~50–100 retrieved candidates per query, offline, once. Train the student on margin-MSE over score differences rather than raw scores — the student can't match the teacher's absolute scale and shouldn't try.
  • Second payoff. The same teacher scores clean your mined negatives. Distillation and denoising come from one offline GPU job.
  • Cost, said out loud. The teacher pass is the bill: queries × candidates forward passes. It is offline, batched, and cacheable — which is precisely the argument for a GPU encoding pipeline.

Frozen vs fine-tuned text encoders

Three positions, with the operational cost attached
ChoiceQualityWhat it really costsPick it when
Frozen base + trained MLP head baseline Cheap. But a generic LM has never seen your catalogue's jargon — sizes, Korean commerce compounds, seller shorthand. Cold start, little labelled data, or you need something in production this quarter.

The versioning trap he may well ask about

Query and item embeddings must come from the same model version. Mixing versions in one index is a silent recall collapse that looks like an index bug — the vectors are valid, the geometry is not. The answer is a shadow index built under the new version, validated offline, then an atomic alias swap. Never an in-place partial rewrite.

Retrieval serving at 10M / 200 QPS / 100 ms

Your ground

This is where Milvus and the GPU encoding pipeline turn into a differentiator: most candidates have read about HNSW, very few have watched one degrade. Lead with arithmetic — it is the fastest possible signal that you have operated this rather than read about it.

Size it before you name an index

Hybrid: lexical + dense

Commit

"Hybrid, fused with RRF, and the reason is not accuracy — it's that BM25 and cosine scores aren't on comparable scales across queries, so a weighted sum needs per-query normalisation that I don't trust."

RRF — rank-based

score(d) = Σ 1 / (k + ranki(d)), k ≈ 60

Scale-free, no tuning, robust to one arm being badly calibrated. Cost: throws away magnitude — a document that BM25 loved at rank 1 and one it merely liked at rank 1 score identically.

Weighted score — value-based

α · norm(BM25) + (1−α) · cos

Keeps magnitude and lets you learn α per query intent (broad vs navigational). Cost: requires per-query normalisation, and α drifts as either arm is retrained. Needs monitoring RRF doesn't.

Where each arm wins, which is the real answer: lexical owns exact identifiers — SKUs, model numbers, brand strings, "5T" — where a dense model will happily return a semantic neighbour and be wrong. Dense owns synonymy, misspellings, and descriptive intent. In a multilingual catalogue (KO / EN / ZH) dense also carries cross-lingual matching that a lexical index cannot without an explicit translation table.

The 100 ms budget, spent

Freshness, cold start, topology

Refresh cadence

Two clocks, and conflating them is the mistake. Item churn (new listings, price, stock) is a streaming insert/tombstone problem — minutes, absorbed by a small incremental segment merged periodically. Model churn is a full 10M re-encode plus rebuild — hours, and it dominates the operational design. At 10M with a GPU encoding pipeline, a full rebuild in ~1–2 hours is achievable, which is what makes weekly model iteration realistic instead of quarterly.

New-item cold start

A dense item tower over content (title, attributes, image) is already the cold-start answer — no interaction history required, which is exactly why it beats ID-embedding recommenders here. Remaining gaps: the item is not in the index yet (fix: a small always-hot "fresh" segment searched in parallel and merged), and its ranking features are all null (fix: back off to category priors, and explicitly flag is_new so the ranker can learn the discount rather than inferring it from missing values).

Shard or replicate

At 10 GB: replicate, don't shard. Sharding buys memory you don't need and costs you fan-out, merge, and a p99 that is the max over shards rather than the mean — with 8 shards you sample the tail 8 times per query. Shard when the index stops fitting, or when filters partition naturally (by category, by region) so a shard becomes a pre-filter and does real work.

Degradation ladder

Have one, and name it: drop efSearch → drop the dense arm and serve lexical-only → serve a cached result for the head query → serve category browse. Each rung is worse relevance and a live page. The candidate who has a degradation ladder has been on call.

03

Ranking for conversion

His org's core

The recruiter named ranking first. Coupang's ranker mixes relevance with attractiveness over 100+ criteria — so this is not "predict CVR," it is "predict several things and combine them under a business objective, calibrated well enough that the combination means something."

Delayed feedback — the CVR-specific problem

A click is observed in milliseconds. A conversion arrives in hours to days. So at training time your most recent data — the data you most need — is systematically mislabelled: an unconverted click may be a true negative or a conversion that hasn't landed yet, and you cannot tell them apart.

Four ways to handle it, worst to best
ApproachMechanismWhat it costs
Wait out the windowOnly train on data older than the attribution window (e.g. 7 days). stale Model is always a week behind. Fatal during a promotion or a seasonal shift.
Delayed-feedback modelJointly model P(convert) and the delay distribution (often exponential); use it to importance-weight partial labels. Assumes a delay family. Breaks when delay is bimodal — impulse buys and considered purchases are not one distribution.
Fake-negative weighted / re-ingestionIngest every click immediately as a negative; when the conversion lands, ingest the positive and correct with importance weights. fresh Streaming complexity and duplicate accounting, but the model stays current. The practical production answer.
Elapsed-time as a featureFeed "time since click" so the model learns the hazard. illegal At serving that feature is always zero. Textbook training/serving skew — and it will look like a brilliant offline result.

ESMM: sample selection bias, structurally

MMoE and PLE

Calibration — why a ranked score still needs it

Commit

"Ranking only needs order, but pCVR isn't only used for ranking — the moment you multiply it by price, blend it with relevance, or threshold it, it has to be a probability. AUC can be flat while calibration collapses."

  • Four consumers that break without it: expected-value sorting (pCVR × margin); the blended objective against relevance and delivery speed — the weights are meaningless if one input drifts in scale; ads and sponsored auctions; and any business rule with a threshold.
  • What breaks it: negative downsampling (mandatory at this scale, and it shifts the prior), distribution shift between train and serve, and any recalibration of an upstream stage.
  • The downsampling correction, cold: if negatives are kept with rate w, then p = p' / (p' + (1 − p')/w).
  • Fit it on a holdout that matches the serving distribution — isotonic when you have volume, Platt when you don't. Isotonic overfits on sparse slices and will silently ruin your tail.
  • Monitor per slice, not globally. Expected-over-observed by category, by position, by device, by new-vs-returning. Global ECE hides exactly the slice that is broken.

Position bias and unbiased LTR

The examination hypothesis: P(click) = P(examined | position) × P(relevant). Train naively on clicks and you learn your own ranker's layout, forever — a self-reinforcing loop where rank 1 is relevant because it was rank 1.

Getting propensities, cheapest to most correct
MethodHowPrice

Intervention harvestingExploit the rank variation you already have — the same (query, item) pair appears at different positions across A/B arms and model versions. Free observational data.free Needs careful logging of which model served which session. Usually the right first move.

The two failure modes to volunteer

Variance. IPW weights are 1/propensity, and deep positions have tiny propensities — one click at rank 40 can dominate a batch. Clip the weights and say you clip them. Position isn't the only bias. Trust bias (users believe the top result more than examination alone explains), presentation bias (image size, badges, "Rocket" tags), and in a grid layout the geometry is 2-D, not a rank. An IPW that models only rank is still biased — it's just less biased.

Real-time vs batch features, and skew

Where the skew comes from

Two implementations of one feature: Spark/SQL for training, a low-latency service for serving. They disagree on nulls, on timezone boundaries, on how a count window closes. Nobody notices, because the offline metric is computed with the offline implementation.

The structural fix

Log at serving, train on the log. Write the exact feature vector the model scored into the training stream, rather than reconstructing it later from the warehouse. This makes skew impossible by construction for the features you log, and it is the single highest-value thing to do in an ML platform.

Point-in-time correctness

The other half. If a feature is "items sold in the last 7 days" and you join today's value onto a three-week-old training row, you have leaked the future into the past. Every feature join needs an as-of timestamp. This is the bug that produces an implausibly good offline result.

What you monitor

Daily distribution comparison between served and trained feature values — PSI or KL per feature, alerting on drift and on null-rate change. Null rate is the one that catches upstream breakage first, because a broken pipeline usually produces nulls before it produces wrong numbers.

Long-tail and ambiguous queries

Say "tail recall" when he asks what you'd own

This is the strongest answer to "what would you want to work on here," because it is a specific problem in their stack rather than a technology. It is also where every technique above has to cooperate.

"toddler winter boots size 5" — worked

Decompose the query before you retrieve for it
TokenConstraint typeConfidenceHow to apply it
bootsProduct typehighHard filter on category. Getting this wrong returns sneakers and destroys trust.
toddlerAge group → implies size range, implies fithighHard filter on age_group, if the attribute has good catalogue coverage. If 40% of listings lack it, filtering deletes 40% of your inventory.
winterSeason / attribute — often only visible in the imagemediumSoft boost. Seller text frequently omits it. This is the multimodal argument arriving on its own.
size 5Variant-level, not item-level. "5T" ≠ "5" ≠ "5 US toddler"mediumNormalise against a size ontology, then filter at the variant level and roll up — an item with a size-5 variant in stock should surface.
Unstated but present: price sensitivity, brand indifference, and — at Coupang — an implicit preference for Rocket delivery that the ranker supplies, not the query.

The rule that matters

Never hard-filter on a low-confidence extraction. A wrong soft boost is a mediocre ranking; a wrong hard filter is a zero-result page, and zero results is the worst outcome in commerce search. Route extraction confidence into the filter/boost decision explicitly, and have a "relax the weakest constraint and retry" path when the filtered candidate count drops below a floor.

What you fall back to when the logs are empty

The defining property of the tail is that per-query learning is impossible — you will never have enough sessions for "toddler winter boots size 5" specifically. So you generalise over query features, not query IDs. That single sentence answers most tail questions, and the concrete ladder is:

  1. Spell and normalisation — a large share of zero-result tail queries are typos or unnormalised units. Cheapest possible win, and always check it first.
  2. Query-to-query retrieval. Embed the query, find the nearest query with dense logs, and inherit its rewrites and attribute extraction. Cheap, online, and it turns a tail query into a head query.
  3. IDF back-off. Drop the least informative token and retry, rather than returning nothing. Order the drop by inverse informativeness, never left-to-right.
  4. Category prediction from the query embedding, then rank within the category using content-based features only — no ID embeddings, because there is no history to embed.
  5. Offline LLM coverage for anything that repeats: see Part 5.

Number to anchor the tail conversation

Query frequency is Zipfian: roughly the top 10⁵ distinct queries cover the large majority of traffic, while the tail is most of the distinct queries and a minority of sessions. That asymmetry is what makes the offline/online split work — you can afford expensive offline computation for the head-and-torso set that covers most traffic, and must be cheap on the rest.

Where the pricing work earns its place

"Most ranking experiments can assume SUTVA. Pricing experiments can't — changing price for one group changes inventory, competitor response, and demand for everyone, so the control is contaminated. I've had to run switchback designs and defend them, and that changes how I read any marketplace experiment: my first question is now whether the units are actually independent."

  • Pre-register: one primary metric, the MDE, the decision rule, and the guardrails — before looking. Everything else is a secondary metric with an FDR correction, and you say so up front.
  • Duration: at least one full week for day-of-week seasonality, regardless of what the power calculation says. Then a second week for novelty.
  • Guardrails at Coupang specifically: p99 latency, zero-result rate, head-query CTR, ads revenue, and delivery-promise mix — because if your relevance win quietly demotes Rocket items, you have moved a metric the company cares about more than yours.
  • Variance reduction: CUPED on a pre-period covariate is nearly free and typically buys a meaningful cut in required sample size. Mention it — it signals you have run experiments that were underpowered and had to do something about it.
  • A flat result is a result. Report the confidence interval, not "no effect." If the interval spans the MDE, the experiment was underpowered and the honest conclusion is "we don't know yet."

Multimodal, and what a query costs

He pokes wide

Product images as the missing attribute channel

Commit

"Images are strongest exactly where seller text is weakest — 'winter', 'lightweight', colour, style. In a marketplace with long-tail sellers and a multilingual catalogue, that's not a nice-to-have, it's the fix for listings whose text is three words long."

  • How: CLIP-style dual encoder contrastively trained on (image, title) pairs straight from the catalogue — free supervision at catalogue scale. Then the step people forget: align the image tower into the query embedding space, or you have built an image-to-text model that your retrieval stack cannot consume.
  • Fusion choice: concatenate-and-project into one item vector (one index, cheap, loses the ability to weight modalities per query) vs. a second index searched in parallel and fused (flexible, doubles the serving cost). At 10M I'd start with a single fused vector and only split if I could show a query class where image should dominate.
  • The GPU story, which is yours: 10M images through a ViT-B is real work — this is the encoding pipeline, and it is where GPU throughput converts directly into iteration speed. A full pass in hours rather than days is the difference between a model you can improve and a model you ship once.
  • Failure modes to volunteer: stale embeddings when sellers swap photos — fix with event-driven re-encode on image change, not a schedule; near-duplicate listings using the same stock photo collapsing to identical vectors — needs dedupe or a diversity penalty; and lifestyle shots that show a model wearing five products, where the image describes the scene rather than the item.

Story 1 · Ambiguity

Shape: a problem where the requirement was genuinely unclear and you had to define it before you could solve it. One sentence of situation, four of what you did, one of impact with a number.

Must contain: how you sized the opportunity before committing, and what you chose not to do as a result. That is Ruthless Prioritization arriving without you naming it.

Ending to rehearse: the estimate you made up front and how close it turned out to be — including if it was wrong.

Story 2 · Cross-team

Shape: you needed another team to change something — a schema, a log, a priority — and had no authority to make them.

Must contain: the argument you used, in their metric not yours; and the steelman of why they initially said no. Influence without Authority plus Company-wide Perspective.

Ending to rehearse: the downstream consumer you protected, and what would have broken for them if you hadn't.

Story 3 · Measured outcome

Shape: shipped, measured, still running. This is the pricing-experimentation one — it is your strongest because the experiment design itself was hard, not just the model.

Must contain: the decision rule set before looking, the guardrails, the confidence interval, the duration, the traffic share. A point estimate with no interval invites the easiest follow-up in the round.

Ending to rehearse: the second-order effect — whose metric moved that you didn't expect.

The second-conversation opener

He screened you himself. Re-telling a story he has already heard reads as a rehearsed pitch. Open by advancing the first conversation instead: "When we spoke you mentioned X — I went and looked at how we handled that, and the thing I got wrong was…"

Three things at once: you listened, you thought about the job between conversations, and you opened on a self-correction — which is the most reliable way to sound senior. Have one real reconsideration prepared. It does not have to be large.

The "I don't know" protocol

A breadth round is designed to find your edge, and it will. Three rules: never bluff a number — arithmetic is the one thing he can check on the spot; never stop at "I don't know", because he still has to fill the time; never over-apologise — one clause, then reason forward. The sentence: "I haven't built that. Here's what I'd expect to be true and how I'd check it —" and then actually say what you'd check. Reasoning to a defensible answer in front of him is a better signal than having memorised it.

What you ask him

Save 6–7 minutes

Ask these in this order. The first two are calibration questions dressed as curiosity; the third is the one he will remember, because most candidates never ask it directly.

"How does the team split between retrieval and ranking — and where's the bigger gap right now?"

Tells you which half of everything above you should be emphasising for the rest of the loop. It also tells you whether the team is organised by stage (retrieval team, ranking team) or by vertical (a team owns a query class end to end), which changes what "owning tail recall" would even mean. Follow-up if he's expansive: "and who owns query understanding — is it its own thing or does it sit with retrieval?"

"What do the first six months look like for whoever takes this? What would you want shipped by month three?"

Two questions in one on purpose. The six-month answer tells you the strategy; the month-three answer tells you whether there is a measurement loop that can even show a result that fast. Pair it with your own ninety-day answer — read the code and the logs, find the measurement gap, ship something small that proves the loop end to end, and only then propose the bigger thing. A six-month redesign in the first ninety days reads as risky.

"You have two requisitions. What separates the L6-2 scope from the L6-1 — in the first year, concretely?"

Ask it directly. He has two seats and four candidates at onsite, and you may as well know which one you are being measured against. Asking shows you understand that scope is the axis — and it hands him the opening to tell you what evidence he is still missing, which you then have the rest of the conversation to supply.

Spares, if there's time

"What does the offline-to-online correlation look like today?" — a manager who can answer this precisely has a healthy measurement culture; one who can't has just told you where your first ninety days go. "How much of the ranking objective is relevance versus attractiveness, and who owns that weighting?" — shows you read their architecture properly. "What's the thing about this team you'd change if you could?" — a long-tenured manager will give you a real answer.

Don't ask him about: work-life balance, promotion timelines, or whether the team uses a particular framework. Not bad questions — better asked of the recruiter.

The night before

Twelve sentences to have ready

If you can say each of these cold, in one breath, the round is covered. Everything else in this document is the material you'd use if he asks for it — and the discipline is offering the depth, not taking it.

Two towers

They stay separate because the item side must be precomputable; the dot product is the only place they touch.

Negatives

In-batch teaches topic, mined hard negatives teach constraints, and the risk in mining is false negatives — so mine at ranks 20–200, not the top.

logQ

Subtract log of the sampling probability during training only; popular items appear as negatives in proportion to popularity, and that's a bias I introduced.

Loss

InfoNCE by default because every negative contributes gradient; triplet only when supervision genuinely arrives as pairs.

Labels

Retrieval trains on clicks and add-to-cart because recall needs coverage; CVR trains on purchases and pays for it with delayed feedback.

Index

10M × 768 int8 plus graph is about 10 GB — replicate it, don't shard it; sharding buys memory I don't need and costs me the p99.

Hybrid

RRF, because BM25 and cosine scores aren't comparable across queries and a weighted sum needs normalisation I don't trust.

Latency

18 query understanding, 25 retrieval with the arms in parallel, 42 ranking, 15 assembly — and I'd budget 70% of it because that's p99.

ESMM

CVR is trained on clicks and applied to impressions; ESMM only ever supervises quantities observable on impressions, so the bias can't enter.

Calibration

Order isn't enough, because pCVR gets multiplied by price and blended with relevance — AUC stays flat while calibration collapses.

LLMs

Everywhere except the request path: offline it manufactures supervision, online I only read what it already wrote.

Experiments

Interleave to kill losers in days, A/B the survivor for the business number — and check the units are independent before either.

The one failure mode for this round

Not being wrong. Still talking forty seconds after you'd already answered. Commit in the first sentence, give the one reason that matters, name the trade-off, stop. The worst outcome is a good technical performance that leaves him unable to picture you on his team — coverage gets you past the bar; specificity about what you would own is what picks you out of four candidates for two seats.
