01

Retrieval training

Highest value

He will poke here because it is the half of Search & Discovery that is hardest to fake. Everything in this part reduces to one sentence: a two-tower model learns exactly what your negatives teach it, and nothing else. The architecture is the easy part.

Two towers, and why they never meet until serving

Offer depth

"The interesting consequence is that the item tower can't see the query, so anything query-conditional has to be pushed into the embedding space geometry. That's why negative mining matters more here than in ranking — want me to go into it?"

Can one tower be deep and the other light?

Commit

"Yes — and usually they should be. The towers have completely different compute budgets, so symmetry is an aesthetic preference, not a requirement. The only thing that has to match is the output space."

This is a good question to be asked, because the answer separates people who have deployed a bi-encoder from people who have read the paper. The asymmetry is not about which side is more important — it is about which side can be batched.

Three axes of asymmetry — they are independent, and people conflate them
AxisOptionsWhat I'd do and why
Depth / capacity Symmetric · light query + heavy item · heavy query + light item Light query, heavy item. Cost is per-query on one side and amortised on the other, and the semantics live on the item side — a title, its attributes and its image carry far more to encode than three typo-laden tokens. The reverse asymmetry is almost always wrong.
Weight sharing Siamese (one shared encoder) · fully separate · shared embeddings with separate stacks Shared subword vocabulary and embedding table, separate encoder stacks. Queries are 2–4 tokens and intent-shaped; titles are 30+ tokens and keyword-stuffed — one encoder handling both underfits the pair. But sharing the vocabulary is what lets "5T" in a query land on "5T" in a title at all.
Modality Text-only both sides · item side multimodal Only the item side has an image to encode, so multimodality is inherently asymmetric. It also makes the item tower the expensive one, which reinforces the first row.

The failure mode of over-asymmetry — volunteer this

If the query tower is too weak it becomes the bottleneck of the shared space, and the big item tower's extra capacity is simply wasted — you pay the GPU bill for representations the query side cannot address. The symptom is diagnostic: item-tower ablations stop moving recall. The fix: train both towers large and jointly, then distil only the query tower into the small one with the item tower frozen, so the space doesn't move under you and you don't have to re-index.

Offer depth — the cache flips the arithmetic

"There's a second-order effect worth mentioning: query frequency is Zipfian, so an embedding cache on the head means the online encoder mostly runs for the tail. At an 80% hit rate your effective online cost drops 5×, which may buy you a deeper query tower than the naïve latency math suggests. The catch is cache invalidation on model change — the cache is versioned with the encoder or it silently serves the old space."

Negative mining: what it actually is

The definition, in one sentence

"A contrastive model never sees a label that says relevant — it only ever sees this one, not those. Negative mining is the choice of those, which means the model learns exactly the distinctions its negatives force it to make, and no others."

The failure everyone has seen follows directly: train on in-batch negatives alone, get a model that matches keywords without matching intent. It nails "boots" and cheerfully returns adult boots for "toddler boots," because no training example ever forced it to separate them. The positives were never the problem.

Hard, soft, semi-hard — the spectrum

If this stays abstract, skip ahead

Part 02 works this entire spectrum on real log rows — one query, eight impressions, named items — and computes what a false negative actually costs in one training step. The concepts below are the same ones; that part just fills in the numbers.

"Hardness" means one thing: how highly the current model already scores this negative. It is therefore defined relative to a checkpoint, which is the fact that generates most of the operational work.

The curriculum, said as a sequence

Epoch 1: in-batch only — learn the coarse geometry. Epoch 2: add category-matched soft negatives. Epoch 3+: add mined hard negatives from the current checkpoint, filtered by a cross-encoder threshold, re-mined every epoch or two (ANCE-style). Starting hard is the classic way to get a model that never converges — the gradients are enormous and mostly point at noise.

Offer depth — "soft negatives" has a second meaning, and it's worth catching

"There are two things people mean by soft. One is semi-hard — a negative of moderate difficulty. The other is a softly labelled negative: instead of a hard zero, it carries the teacher's relevance score. That second one is strictly more information and it is the real cure for false negatives — a mined negative that is secretly relevant gets a high teacher score, so it stops being treated as a zero instead of being thrown away. If you have a teacher, soft labels beat any filtering threshold."

The three things to say about hard negatives, in order

1. Where from. Mine with the current model against the full index (ANCE-style), refreshed every epoch or two — negatives mined once against the initial model go stale and stop being hard. 2. Which rank band. Not top-10 — take ranks ~20–200. The top of the list is where false negatives live. 3. How many. Too many and training destabilises: the model collapses toward separating near-duplicates and loses coarse structure. One to four per positive, plus full in-batch, is the working range.

Offer depth — denoising

"The clean fix for false negatives is to score mined candidates with the cross-encoder teacher and drop anything above a threshold. You're already paying for that teacher if you're distilling, so it's nearly free."

Sampled softmax and the logQ correction

The full softmax denominator runs over 10M items. Sampled softmax replaces it with a sample — but negatives sampled from the click stream appear in proportion to their popularity. The model therefore learns to push popular items down, because it sees them as negatives far more often than chance. That is a bias you introduced, not a property of the data.

The correction, said precisely

Score used in the loss: s'(q,i) = s(q,i) − log Q(i), where Q(i) is the probability item i is drawn as a negative. In practice Q is estimated by streaming frequency with a decaying count-min sketch, not recomputed in batch. Applied at training only — removed at serving. Leaving it in at serving is a real and common bug; it shows up as popular items mysteriously under-retrieved.

Offer depth

"There's a judgement call underneath it: logQ corrects the sampling bias, but popularity is also genuine signal in commerce. Fully correcting can hurt engagement metrics. I'd treat the correction strength as a tunable and let the A/B decide, rather than assuming the unbiased estimator is the business-optimal one."

Cross-encoder → bi-encoder distillation

Commit

"Distillation is the highest-leverage thing you can do to a two-tower model, because it converts a one-positive label into a fully-scored candidate list — it fixes the supervision, not the architecture."

  • Teacher. Cross-encoder over (query, title + attributes), trained on human labels plus engagement. It can attend across the pair, so it knows "5T" and "toddler size 5" are the same constraint.
  • Transfer. Score the teacher over ~50–100 retrieved candidates per query, offline, once. Train the student on margin-MSE over score differences rather than raw scores — the student can't match the teacher's absolute scale and shouldn't try.
  • Second payoff. The same teacher scores clean your mined negatives. Distillation and denoising come from one offline GPU job.
  • Cost, said out loud. The teacher pass is the bill: queries × candidates forward passes. It is offline, batched, and cacheable — which is precisely the argument for a GPU encoding pipeline.

The versioning trap he may well ask about

Query and item embeddings must come from the same model version. Mixing versions in one index is a silent recall collapse that looks like an index bug — the vectors are valid, the geometry is not. The answer is a shadow index built under the new version, validated offline, then an atomic alias swap. Never an in-place partial rewrite.

03

Losses, disentangled

Stop guessing

These get conflated constantly, including in papers. The way out is to stop treating them as a menu of alternatives and notice they differ on two independent axes: the shape of the supervision you have, and what you need the output number to mean. Place a loss on those two axes and when to use it stops being a memory problem.

The sentence that resolves most of the confusion

"InfoNCE and cross-entropy aren't alternatives — InfoNCE is softmax cross-entropy, where the classes are the candidate items you chose for that step. That's why negative mining is a modelling decision in retrieval and not in ordinary classification: you're picking the class set."

Axis 1 — what shape is your supervision?

The family, one at a time

Binary cross-entropy · log loss

L = −[ y·log p + (1−y)·log(1−p) ]

Pointwise, probability-valued. The default for CTR and CVR.

Why it beats MSE here, precisely: through a sigmoid, ∂L/∂logit = (p − y) — clean and proportional to the error. MSE through a sigmoid gives (p − y)·p·(1−p), which vanishes exactly when the model is confidently wrong. Both are proper scoring rules, so the difference is optimisation, not correctness — and that distinction is worth making out loud.

Softmax cross-entropy

L = −log( e^{s_y} / Σ_k e^{s_k} )

Listwise, over K mutually exclusive classes. Correct, unbiased, and O(K) — which is why it's fine for category prediction over a few thousand classes and impossible over a 10 M catalogue.

Everything below is a way of making this loss affordable or reshaping what the "classes" are.

Sampled softmax + logQ

s'(q,i) = s(q,i) − log Q(i)

Not a different loss — softmax CE with the denominator estimated from a sample. The sample is drawn from the click stream, so popular items appear as negatives far more often than chance, and the estimate is biased.

logQ is the de-biasing term, not a loss. Subtract each sampled item's log sampling probability from its logit. Training only — leaving it in at serving under-retrieves popular items.

InfoNCE · NT-Xent

L = −log( e^{s⁺/τ} / Σ e^{s/τ} )

Softmax CE where the classes are the candidates. The class set changes every step and you construct it — which is the entire reason negative mining exists.

τ is a hardness knob you already have. Small τ concentrates the loss on the single hardest negative (approaching a max); large τ weights all negatives equally. You can get some of hard-mining's effect by lowering τ, with none of the false-negative risk — worth saying.

Contrastive loss — the original pair loss

L = y·d² + (1−y)·max(0, m − d)²

Pairwise, distance-valued. Pull matching pairs to zero distance; push non-matching pairs past margin m.

Notice what's odd: it demands positives be identical, which is stronger than any ranking task needs and tends to over-collapse the space. Triplet exists to relax exactly this.

The terminology trap

When anyone says "contrastive learning" today they almost always mean InfoNCE-style training, not the pair loss above. Two different things share the word.

What to do: clarify once, cheaply — "do you mean the margin pair loss, or InfoNCE-style?" That reads as precision, not as dodging. Guessing and answering the wrong one is the only way to lose this exchange.

Triplet loss

L = max(0, d(a,p) − d(a,n) + m)

Pairwise, relative. Only asks that the positive be closer than the negative by a margin — the fix for contrastive loss's absolute demand.

Three consequences to volunteer: once a triplet satisfies the margin its gradient is exactly zero, so mining is mandatory by construction, not an optimisation. It uses one negative per step where InfoNCE uses all of them, which is the main reason retrieval moved on. And m lives in the units of your embedding space — carrying a margin over from a previous project is a real and common bug.

BPR / pairwise logistic (RankNet)

L = −log σ(s⁺ − s⁻)

Pairwise, smooth, no margin to tune — which is its main advantage over triplet. The workhorse for implicit feedback in recommenders.

And note what it actually is: cross-entropy again, applied to the difference of two scores. Most of this list is cross-entropy wearing a different coat.

LambdaRank / LambdaMART

Listwise, and the clever one. NDCG isn't differentiable, so instead of defining a loss and taking its gradient, you define the gradient directly: a pairwise gradient scaled by how much swapping that pair would change NDCG.

This is LightGBM's lambdarank objective — worth naming, because the JD names LightGBM, and GBDT with LambdaMART is still a very strong L2 ranker on dense features.

MSE / L2

L = (ŷ − y)²

Pointwise, continuous-valued. Correct when the target genuinely is continuous — dwell time, predicted GMV, price elasticity, or a teacher's score.

Wrong for a 0/1 event you need calibrated, for the vanishing-gradient reason above. This is the single most common misuse of the four you named.

Margin-MSE — distillation

L = ‖ (s⁺−s⁻) − (t⁺−t⁻) ‖²

Pairwise in shape, regression in value. MSE on score differences, and the difference is the whole trick: the student can't reproduce the teacher's absolute scale but it can reproduce its margins.

This is why MSE reappears in retrieval despite everything above — it isn't predicting an event, it's matching a teacher.

KL divergence · soft labels

Match the teacher's whole distribution over candidates rather than a pair of scores. Strictly more information than margin-MSE, and it is the clean fix for false negatives: a mined negative that's secretly relevant gets a high teacher probability, so it stops being treated as a zero rather than having to be filtered out.

Cost: sensitive to the teacher's temperature, and you need the teacher's full candidate scores, not just top-1.

One more you may get asked about — focal loss

Cross-entropy with a (1−p)^γ factor that down-weights easy examples; it comes up for extreme class imbalance, which CVR certainly has. The caveat to volunteer: it deliberately distorts the loss surface, so it decalibrates — and since pCVR gets multiplied by price, you now owe a recalibration step you didn't owe before. Often the simpler answer is negative downsampling plus the analytic correction.

Hybrid: lexical + dense

Commit

RRF — rank-based

score(d) = Σ 1 / (k + ranki(d)), k ≈ 60

Scale-free, no tuning, robust to one arm being badly calibrated. Cost: throws away magnitude — a document that BM25 loved at rank 1 and one it merely liked at rank 1 score identically.

Weighted score — value-based

α · norm(BM25) + (1−α) · cos

Keeps magnitude and lets you learn α per query intent (broad vs navigational). Cost: requires per-query normalisation, and α drifts as either arm is retrained. Needs monitoring RRF doesn't.

Where each arm wins, which is the real answer: lexical owns exact identifiers — SKUs, model numbers, brand strings, "5T" — where a dense model will happily return a semantic neighbour and be wrong. Dense owns synonymy, misspellings, and descriptive intent. In a multilingual catalogue (KO / EN / ZH) dense also carries cross-lingual matching that a lexical index cannot without an explicit translation table.

The 100 ms budget, spent

Freshness, cold start, topology

Refresh cadence

Two clocks, and conflating them is the mistake. Item churn (new listings, price, stock) is a streaming insert/tombstone problem — minutes, absorbed by a small incremental segment merged periodically. Model churn is a full 10M re-encode plus rebuild — hours, and it dominates the operational design. At 10M with a GPU encoding pipeline, a full rebuild in ~1–2 hours is achievable, which is what makes weekly model iteration realistic instead of quarterly.

New-item cold start

A dense item tower over content (title, attributes, image) is already the cold-start answer — no interaction history required, which is exactly why it beats ID-embedding recommenders here. Remaining gaps: the item is not in the index yet (fix: a small always-hot "fresh" segment searched in parallel and merged), and its ranking features are all null (fix: back off to category priors, and explicitly flag is_new so the ranker can learn the discount rather than inferring it from missing values).

Shard or replicate

At 10 GB: replicate, don't shard. Sharding buys memory you don't need and costs you fan-out, merge, and a p99 that is the max over shards rather than the mean — with 8 shards you sample the tail 8 times per query. Shard when the index stops fitting, or when filters partition naturally (by category, by region) so a shard becomes a pre-filter and does real work.

Degradation ladder

Have one, and name it: drop efSearch → drop the dense arm and serve lexical-only → serve a cached result for the head query → serve category browse. Each rung is worse relevance and a live page. The candidate who has a degradation ladder has been on call.

The index families, mechanically

Name it last

The question always sounds like "which index would you pick," so people answer with a name. It is actually "show me the recall–latency–memory triangle for this corpus." Answer with arithmetic, then the mechanism, then the name — in that order.

The clarification that reframes the whole conversation

"These aren't one list — they're two independent axes. LSH, IVF and HNSW are routing: how you avoid looking at everything. Scalar quantisation, PQ and OPQ are compression: how you make each vector cheaper to hold and score. You pick one from each column. So 'HNSW vs IVF-PQ' is really 'graph + int8' vs 'clusters + product codes' — and you can absolutely build HNSW-over-PQ or IVF-over-int8."

Step zero — do you need an index at all?

Compute the flat-scan ceiling before naming anything. A modern core scans memory for dot products at roughly 10 GB/s with SIMD. In 10 ms that is ~100 MB, which at 768 int8 bytes per vector is:

The flat-scan baseline, out loud

~130,000 vectors per core per 10 ms — about 2 M vectors on a 16-core box.
Under ~1 M vectors per shard: don't build an index. Flat scan gives perfect recall, no parameters, no rebuild story, and — the part people miss — no filter problem at all. An enormous amount of engineering has been spent on indexes for corpora that fit in this box.
At 10 M × 768 int8, a full scan is 7.7 GB per query — ~770 ms on one core, ~50 ms across 16. That is over budget, but only by about 3×, which is exactly why at this scale almost any index works and the real engineering is freshness and filters.
At 100 M it is 77 GB per query — seconds. Three orders of magnitude past, which also tells you a single 100× speedup won't be enough and you'll be sharding too.

LSH — the one with guarantees and no users

Mechanism

A hash family where close points collide more often than far ones. For cosine that's SimHash: draw a random hyperplane, take the sign of the dot product, keep one bit. Collision probability is exactly 1 − θ/π.

Amplify: AND over K bits inside a table (raises precision, lowers recall), OR over L tables (raises recall, costs memory and time linearly). Query: hash, take the union of the L buckets, re-rank those candidates exactly.

What it costs you

With ρ = ln p₁ / ln p₂, query time is O(d·mρ) and space is O(m1+ρ). That superlinear space bound is the quiet killer — you hold L copies of the id list. At 10 M with L = 20 that's ~3 GB of tables on top of the vectors you still need for re-ranking.

The honest verdict: the guarantees are real, the constants are bad, and graphs beat it empirically at every operating point anyone cares about.

Where it still genuinely wins

It's data-independent. No training, no k-means, no graph build — so no rebuild when the distribution shifts, and inserts are O(L) with no structural damage.

That makes it the right tool for near-duplicate detection — which at Coupang is a real problem: many sellers listing the same product with the same stock photo. Dedupe and clustering at ingest is an LSH job, not a retrieval job.

How to handle it in the round

"I'd name LSH because the theory is the only one with provable guarantees, and then not propose it — superlinear space and worse empirical Pareto than a graph. Where I would reach for it is near-duplicate detection on the catalogue, where its data-independence is the feature."

Knowing why you rejected something is worth more than knowing what you'd pick.

IVF — partition, then scan a few partitions

Mechanism: k-means the corpus into C clusters and keep the centroids. At query time, compute distance to all C centroids, pick the nprobe nearest, and scan those posting lists exhaustively.

  • The cost is C + nprobe·(m/C) distance computations, which is minimised at C = √m. At 10 M that's ~3,200 clusters. Being able to derive C rather than quote a default is the whole signal here.
  • Centroids are free: 3,200 × 768 × 4 B ≈ 10 MB. IVF's memory cost is essentially zero; whatever you're storing, you'd be storing anyway.
  • Build is cheap — one k-means on a sample, minutes. This matters more than it sounds: it is the difference between rebuilding nightly and rebuilding fortnightly.
  • nprobe is the query-time recall knob. At nprobe = C you've reconstructed flat scan, so recall is bounded only by the compression you chose, not by IVF itself.

Three IVF pathologies — naming these is the experience signal

Cluster imbalance. k-means on real catalogue data produces clusters differing by orders of magnitude in size, so the same nprobe buys wildly different work per query — and your p99 is set by the fat clusters, not the average.
Boundary queries. A query sitting near a Voronoi boundary needs a much larger nprobe than one sitting on a centroid, and nothing in the index knows which you have.
Norm imbalance under MIPS. Plain k-means clusters by direction, but under inner product the answer depends on magnitude too — so the partition is optimising for the wrong geometry.

PQ — compression, and the only source of a hard recall ceiling

Mechanism: split the 768-d vector into M subvectors, run k-means in each subspace to 256 centroids, and store M bytes — one centroid id per subspace. At M = 96 that's 8 dimensions per subvector and 96 bytes instead of 3,072: 32×.

  • Scoring never decompresses. Asymmetric distance computation (ADC): precompute one M × 256 lookup table per query, then each candidate costs M table lookups and adds. That's why PQ is fast as well as small.
  • OPQ learns a rotation first so variance spreads evenly across subspaces. Nearly free at query time and worth several points of recall — if you say "PQ" you should say "with OPQ."
  • Anisotropic / ScaNN-style is the one to name if the objective is inner product: error parallel to the vector damages the inner product far more than orthogonal error, so the quantiser should weight them differently rather than minimising plain reconstruction error.
  • Scalar int8 first, always. A free 4× with negligible recall loss — calibrate the scale per dimension, not globally. Only reach for PQ when int8 still doesn't fit.

The one sentence about PQ and recall

"PQ is the only thing in this stack that puts a ceiling on recall — the scores themselves are approximate, so no amount of nprobe recovers it. The fix is to re-rank the top few hundred with uncompressed vectors, which recovers most of the ceiling for almost nothing, and which is why 'IVF-PQ' without a re-rank tier is usually a misconfiguration rather than a design."

HNSW — the production default, and why

Mechanism, in two sentences worth having: greedy traversal on the Delaunay graph is exact, but the Delaunay graph's degree explodes with dimension — so every practical graph is an approximation of it: a k-NN graph, pruned by a relative-neighbourhood rule to stay navigable at low degree. HNSW then adds a hierarchy of long-range links, which is Kleinberg's small-world construction and buys logarithmic hop counts.

Why graph search is memory-bound, not compute-bound — a strong thing to volunteer

At efSearch = 128 you touch a few thousand candidates. The arithmetic is ~3,000 × 768 int8 with SIMD ≈ 30 µs. The cache misses are ~3,000 random accesses × ~80 ns ≈ 240 µs. Pointer chasing is 8× the arithmetic.
Two consequences: quantising to int8 helps more than the 4× memory saving suggests, because it is also 4× fewer bytes per cache miss; and adding cores stops helping long before you expect it to.

Deletes — the HNSW weakness worth knowing cold

You cannot really remove a node from a graph: other nodes route through it, so removing it severs paths and degrades recall for queries that have nothing to do with the deleted item. So everyone tombstones — mark deleted, keep it for routing, filter it from results. Three consequences: the index only grows, with churn rather than corpus size; effective efSearch silently falls, because some fraction of what you examine can't be returned (at 20% tombstones you're doing 20% more work for the same recall); and therefore you rebuild on a cadence sized from the churn rate. Inserts, by contrast, are fine — an HNSW insert is itself a search plus a link.

Vamana / DiskANN is the variant to name when memory is tight but SSDs aren't: it prunes with a slack factor α > 1, is the only practical graph with worst-case guarantees, and is designed to keep PQ codes in RAM for routing while full vectors live on disk. That's the answer to "what if it doesn't fit and I can't accept PQ's ceiling."

Offer depth — "how would you estimate selectivity?"

"The same way a query planner does: per-value counts for the low-cardinality fields, a sketch for the rest, combine assuming independence, and hand-correct the pairs you know are correlated — in-stock and category are not independent on a marketplace. The trap in the whole topic

The question looks like "which index would you pick," so people answer with a name. It is actually "show me the recall–latency–memory triangle for this specific corpus, and then tell me what your filters and your update rate do to it." Pick the index last. And never benchmark on synthetic random vectors — in high dimensions with independent coordinates every point is an approximate nearest neighbour, so the ground truth is meaningless and so is any recall number measured against it. Real embeddings are searchable because they're clustered and have low intrinsic dimension; that is the only reason any of this works.

The interaction ladder

Where DCN and friends live

Every architecture question in search collapses onto one axis: how much query–item interaction can you afford at this stage? Answer that and the model families sort themselves — including the one he is most likely to name, which is DCN. This is also the cleanest possible answer to "why can't you just use your best model everywhere."

L0retrieval
No interaction — the score must factorise

10 M docs · two-tower, dot product only. The item vector cannot depend on the query, because it was computed last Tuesday. Anything that mixes a query feature with an item feature before the dot product is banned here — not because it performs worse, but because it makes precomputation impossible.

L1pre-rank
Late or shallow interaction — a little mixing, cheaply

~2,000 docs · ~0.04 ms each. Three legitimate designs: a small DNN over a handful of cross features; a late-interaction model that keeps most of the item side precomputed; or a distilled student of L2. The constraint that defines this rung is that most of the computation still has to be cacheable — you cannot afford 2,000 full forward passes.

L2rank
Early interaction, feature level — this is where DCN lives

~300 docs · full feature vector per doc. Wide & Deep, DeepFM, DCN-v2, AutoInt, DIN/DIEN, GBDT — plus an MMoE or PLE head on top. Nothing is precomputable: every score is a fresh forward pass over (user × query × item) features.

L3re-rank
Full token interaction, and page-level effects

~50 docs. A cross-encoder that reads query and title together, and the list-level concerns that only exist once you know the whole page: diversity, seller deduplication, ads blending, and the delivery-promise mix.

Commit line if he asks "where does DCN fit?"

"L2 ranking, and the reason is structural rather than empirical: a cross network builds explicit multiplicative interactions between user/query features and item features. That is exactly the thing you cannot factorise into a dot product — so it can't be precomputed, so it can't be retrieval."

Two traps in this family

The position feature. Wide & Deep and DCN implementations routinely feed display position as a feature so the model can absorb position bias. At serving that feature doesn't exist — you set it to a constant (position 1, or the training mean). Get this wrong and you have a spectacular offline result and a dead A/B, which is the same training-serving skew story as the elapsed-time feature in the next part.

The tail. Wide & Deep's wide half memorises pairs it has seen. On Coupang's tail there is nothing to memorise, so the deep/embedding half is doing 100% of the work. That is the same sentence as "generalise over features, not IDs" — and it is the argument for why tail investment is an embedding problem, not a feature-engineering problem.

"Dense tabular features, modest cardinality, limited data — GBDT, and I wouldn't apologise for it.   • Four consumers that break without it: expected-value sorting (pCVR × margin); the blended objective against relevance and delivery speed — the weights are meaningless if one input drifts in scale; ads and sponsored auctions; and any business rule with a threshold.
  • What breaks it: negative downsampling (mandatory at this scale, and it shifts the prior), distribution shift between train and serve, and any recalibration of an upstream stage.
  • The downsampling correction, cold: if negatives are kept with rate w, then p = p' / (p' + (1 − p')/w).
  • Fit it on a holdout that matches the serving distribution — isotonic when you have volume, Platt when you don't. Isotonic overfits on sparse slices and will silently ruin your tail.
  • Monitor per slice, not globally. Expected-over-observed by category, by position, by device, by new-vs-returning. Global ECE hides exactly the slice that is broken.

Position bias, IPW and unbiased LTR

The examination hypothesis: P(click) = P(examined | position) × P(relevant). Train naively on clicks and you learn your own ranker's layout, forever — a self-reinforcing loop where rank 1 is relevant because it was rank 1.

The two failure modes to volunteer

Variance. IPW weights are 1/propensity, and deep positions have tiny propensities — one click at rank 40 can dominate a batch. Clip the weights and say you clip them. Position isn't the only bias. Trust bias (users believe the top result more than examination alone explains), presentation bias (image size, badges, "Rocket" tags), and in a grid layout the geometry is 2-D, not a rank. An IPW that models only rank is still biased — it's just less biased.

Real-time vs batch features, and skew

Where the skew comes from

Two implementations of one feature: Spark/SQL for training, a low-latency service for serving. They disagree on nulls, on timezone boundaries, on how a count window closes. Nobody notices, because the offline metric is computed with the offline implementation.

The structural fix

Log at serving, train on the log. Write the exact feature vector the model scored into the training stream, rather than reconstructing it later from the warehouse. This makes skew impossible by construction for the features you log, and it is the single highest-value thing to do in an ML platform.

Point-in-time correctness

The other half. If a feature is "items sold in the last 7 days" and you join today's value onto a three-week-old training row, you have leaked the future into the past. Every feature join needs an as-of timestamp. This is the bug that produces an implausibly good offline result.

What you monitor

Daily distribution comparison between served and trained feature values — PSI or KL per feature, alerting on drift and on null-rate change. Null rate is the one that catches upstream breakage first, because a broken pipeline usually produces nulls before it produces wrong numbers.

The rule that matters

Never hard-filter on a low-confidence extraction. A wrong soft boost is a mediocre ranking; a wrong hard filter is a zero-result page, and zero results is the worst outcome in commerce search. Route extraction confidence into the filter/boost decision explicitly, and have a "relax the weakest constraint and retry" path when the filtered candidate count drops below a floor.

What you fall back to when the logs are empty

The defining property of the tail is that per-query learning is impossible — you will never have enough sessions for "toddler winter boots size 5" specifically. So you generalise over query features, not query IDs. That single sentence answers most tail questions, and the concrete ladder is:

  1. Spell and normalisation — a large share of zero-result tail queries are typos or unnormalised units. Cheapest possible win, and always check it first.
  2. Query-to-query retrieval. Embed the query, find the nearest query with dense logs, and inherit its rewrites and attribute extraction. Cheap, online, and it turns a tail query into a head query.
  3. IDF back-off. Drop the least informative token and retry, rather than returning nothing. Order the drop by inverse informativeness, never left-to-right.
  4. Category prediction from the query embedding, then rank within the category using content-based features only — no ID embeddings, because there is no history to embed.
  5. Offline LLM coverage for anything that repeats: see Part 5.

Number to anchor the tail conversation

Query frequency is Zipfian: roughly the top 10⁵ distinct queries cover the large majority of traffic, while the tail is most of the distinct queries and a minority of sessions. That asymmetry is what makes the offline/online split work — you can afford expensive offline computation for the head-and-torso set that covers most traffic, and must be cheap on the rest.

The decision that actually matters: when do you write to the catalogue?

Every attribute prediction becomes a threshold decision, and the threshold is not a modelling parameter — it is a product one, set by the cost of being wrong for that specific attribute.

What changes at Walmart scale

Beyond roughly 100 M items and millions of sellers, four things stop being modelling problems:

Provenance becomes the schema

Every attribute value carries source (seller / model / human), confidence, model_version and timestamp. Without it you cannot roll back a bad model release, cannot measure whether the model or the seller was right, and downstream teams cannot set thresholds appropriate to their own risk. This is the single most consequential design decision in the programme, and it is a schema decision, not an ML one.

Attributes become a shared dependency

Search filters, ranking features, ads targeting, recommendations and fulfilment all read the same columns. So a precision regression is five teams' incident, and a coverage improvement is five teams' win. That is Company-wide Perspective arriving in technical clothing — and it's why the contract with consumers is a published confidence, not a bare value.

The seller loop is the cheapest model you have

A "your listing is missing season — is this a winter boot?" prompt at upload time, pre-filled with the model's guess, converts far better than anything a re-trained backbone will buy you. The highest-leverage CV output is often a suggestion, not a write. Saying this reads as someone who has run a catalogue programme rather than a model.

Two populations, two strategies

Coupang-specific and worth landing: Rocket inventory is first-party — physical access, controlled studio images, measured dimensions, so attributes can be near-complete. Marketplace 3P listings are seller-supplied and messy. Those are different coverage targets and different thresholds, and treating them as one population is how the aggregate metric hides the broken half.

The cost arithmetic, if he asks

10 M items × ~6 images ≈ 60 M images per full pass. A ViT-B at roughly 500 images/s/GPU is ~120,000 GPU-seconds — about 33 GPU-hours, call it a day on a small node or an hour on a modest fleet. That is affordable annually and painful weekly, which is precisely the argument for the cascade: the expensive model only on new, changed, high-traffic and low-confidence items, and a distilled model everywhere else. Order of magnitude — say it as an estimate.

The two-minute version, if he only gives you two minutes

"Images are strongest exactly where seller text is weakest — season, colour, pattern, style — and in a multilingual catalogue they're also language-independent, so they're how a Korean-only listing becomes searchable in English. The hard part isn't the model, it's that the attribute schema is category-conditional, so you can't hand-label it: you bootstrap from the existing catalogue, propagate across stock-photo duplicate groups, and spend your human labels on evaluation and thresholds. And the decision that actually matters is when you write to the catalogue — auto-apply, suggest to the seller, or send to review — with a per-attribute threshold set by the cost of being wrong."

The trap

Answering this question with an architecture. "CLIP-style dual encoder, ViT-B backbone, contrastive on (image, title) pairs" is correct and says nothing — every candidate has that. The differentiated answer is the operational half: what you do when the image and the text disagree, what stops you propagating a size across a stock-photo group, and who gets paged when precision drops. That is also where Dive Deep gets scored.

It also quietly signals that you're thinking about staying.

"What are you personally spending most of your time on right now?"

Disarming, and people like answering it. What it actually gets you is his current worry — which is, almost by definition, the thing you'd be walking into. If his answer and the job description disagree, believe his answer.

"What's the thing about this team you'd change if you could?"

An invitation to be honest, and most long-tenured managers take it. If the answer is a real constraint — headcount, a legacy system, a measurement gap — you've learned where the friction is. If the answer is "nothing," that is also information.

"What would make you look back in a year and say this hire went really well?"

Closes the conversation by inviting him to state the bar explicitly, which is useful to you and flattering to him. It also hands you the exact line to reference in your follow-up note. Ask it last.

What not to ask him

Work-life balance, promotion timelines, and whether the team uses a particular framework. None are bad questions — all are better asked of the recruiter, who is paid to answer them and won't read them as a signal. The operational-load question above is the safe way to get at the first one, because it's framed as scope.

Also skip anything you could have answered yourself from the job posting or their engineering blog. In a round where specificity is the whole tell, a question with a public answer costs you more than not asking one.

Reading the answers

Green: specific, slightly weary, includes a number or a name. Someone who has lived it. Yellow: fluent but abstract — describes how it should work. Red: reframes your question into a different, easier one. That last pattern, on the guardrail question in particular, is the one worth noticing — it usually means the answer is "nobody decides, it just ships."

The night before

Twenty-six sentences to have ready

If you can say each of these cold, in one breath, the round is covered. Everything else in this document is the material you'd use if he asks for it — and the discipline is offering the depth, not taking it.

Two towers

They stay separate because the item side must be precomputable; the dot product is the only place they touch.

Asymmetry

Light query tower, heavy item tower — not because the item matters more, but because the item side batches offline and the query side is batch-one and latency-bound.

Negatives

In-batch teaches topic, mined hard negatives teach constraints, and the risk in mining is false negatives — so mine at ranks 20–200, not the top.

Hard vs soft

Hardness is defined by the current model, so it goes stale; soft negatives give steady gradient with low false-negative risk, hard ones give the big gradient and all the danger.

logQ

Subtract log of the sampling probability during training only; popular items appear as negatives in proportion to popularity, and that's a bias I introduced.

Loss

InfoNCE by default because every negative contributes gradient; triplet only when supervision genuinely arrives as pairs.

InfoNCE = CE

InfoNCE is softmax cross-entropy with the candidate set as the classes — which is why picking the negatives is a modelling decision and not a data-loading one.

Triplet vs contrastive

Contrastive pair loss asks positives to be at distance zero; triplet only asks them to be closer — and a satisfied triplet has zero gradient, so mining is mandatory.

MSE

Continuous targets or matching a teacher's margins — never a 0/1 event you need calibrated, because through a sigmoid its gradient vanishes exactly when the model is confidently wrong.

Labels

Retrieval trains on clicks and add-to-cart because recall needs coverage; CVR trains on purchases and pays for it with delayed feedback.

Label values

Derived from log-odds of human-judged relevance per action, not picked by taste — and they come out flatter than intuition: a purchase is about 2.6 clicks, not ten.

False negatives

On a real candidate list, mining the top of it spends most of the push-down gradient on items the query genuinely wants — which is why you mine at ranks 20–200 and denoise with a teacher.

Downsampling

Keep negatives at rate w and the model over-predicts; q = p/(p+(1−p)/w) puts it back. At w=0.1 a raw 0.5 is really 0.09.

DCN

L2 ranking, structurally — a cross network mixes query features with item features, which is exactly what you can't factorise into a dot product.

Two axes

Routing and compression are separate choices — LSH/IVF/HNSW decide what you look at, int8/PQ decide how big each vector is, and re-ranking is a third decision people forget.

Flat first

Under a million vectors per shard, don't build an index at all: exact, no parameters, no rebuild, no filter problem.

PQ ceiling

PQ is the only thing that caps recall — nprobe can't cross it, and re-ranking the top few hundred with full vectors recovers most of it for almost nothing.

Index

10M × 768 int8 plus graph is about 10 GB — replicate it, don't shard it; sharding buys memory I don't need and costs me the p99.

Hybrid

RRF, because BM25 and cosine scores aren't comparable across queries and a weighted sum needs normalisation I don't trust.

Latency

18 query understanding, 25 retrieval with the arms in parallel, 42 ranking, 15 assembly — and I'd budget 70% of it because that's p99.

ESMM

CVR is trained on clicks and applied to impressions; ESMM only ever supervises quantities observable on impressions, so the bias can't enter.

Calibration

Order isn't enough, because pCVR gets multiplied by price and blended with relevance — AUC stays flat while calibration collapses.

LLMs

Everywhere except the request path: offline it manufactures supervision, online I only read what it already wrote.

Catalogue CV

Images are strongest where seller text is weakest — season, colour, pattern — and they are language-independent, which in a Korean/English/Mandarin catalogue is how a Korean-only listing becomes searchable in English.

Attribute thresholds

The decision that matters is when you write to the catalogue: auto-apply, suggest to the seller, or send to review — threshold set per attribute by the cost of being wrong, not by F1.

Experiments

Interleave to kill losers in days, A/B the survivor for the business number — and check the units are independent before either.

The one failure mode for this round

Not being wrong. Still talking forty seconds after you'd already answered. Commit in the first sentence, give the one reason that matters, name the trade-off, stop. The worst outcome is a good technical performance that leaves him unable to picture you on his team — coverage gets you past the bar; specificity about what you would own is what picks you out of four candidates for two seats.
