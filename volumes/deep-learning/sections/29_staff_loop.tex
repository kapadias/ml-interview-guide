\chapter{The Staff Loop}
\label{chap:staff_loop}

\begin{tldr}
This is the meta-chapter: not what to know, but how the loop that tests it actually works. Every serious ML loop---frontier lab or platform company---tests the same five surfaces under different round names: breadth, depth, coding, system design, and behavioral/staff signals. The chapter maps those surfaces to their prep locations in this program, then spends its core on the thing no knowledge chapter teaches: what separates an L5 answer from an L6 answer from an L7 answer \textit{to the same question}, shown through three fully worked before/after pairs. It then covers the craft of the project deep dive (the round that produces the most staff-level rejections), the meta-skill of staff system design, behavioral and values rounds, research-taste rounds at labs, day-of execution mechanics, and the offer stage. Read this chapter first to see the whole board; read it again the week before your loop.
\end{tldr}

\section{Anatomy of the Loop}
\label{sec:loop_anatomy}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Five Surfaces, Many Round Names}

Companies name their rounds differently---``ML depth,'' ``domain discussion,'' ``craft interview,'' ``technical deep dive''---but every staff-level ML loop in 2026 is sampling the same five surfaces:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Breadth.} Do you have the whole standard map at one-to-two levels of depth, or are you a specialist with cliffs? Increasingly folded into depth and design rounds at frontier labs rather than run standalone; still a named round at platform companies and in phone screens.
    \item \textbf{Depth.} In your claimed specialty, is your knowledge load-bearing or recited? Probed Socratically: every answer spawns a ``why,'' a ``what breaks,'' or a ``derive it.''
    \item \textbf{Coding.} Two flavors: hands-on ML coding (implement attention, debug a training script) and general software engineering (build a small system, find the deadlock, profile the pipeline). Frontier labs hire staff MLEs as engineers first; most loops contain at least one round with no ML in it.
    \item \textbf{System design.} Training-side or serving-side ML infrastructure, or applied product design where the model is a component. The primary leveling instrument at staff level.
    \item \textbf{Behavioral and staff signals.} Ownership, influence without authority, judgment under ambiguity, mentoring---plus the project deep dive, which is the loop's authenticity check on everything else.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keyinsight}[Round Names Vary; the Surfaces Do Not]
Do not prepare for round names. Prepare the five surfaces, then ask your recruiter to map their loop's names onto them---recruiters will do this happily and accurately. The mapping question that matters: which rounds are \textit{gates} (pass/fail: the coding screen, a values conversation) and which are \textit{calibrators} (they decide level, not hire/no-hire: design, the deep dive, behavioral). Gates get you to the committee; calibrators decide what the committee offers.
\end{keyinsight}

\section{Level Calibration: The Same Question at L5, L6, and L7}
\label{sec:level_calibration}
%==============================================================================

Leveling is not decided by what you know. Interviewers at every company report the same experience: two candidates give factually equivalent answers and land two levels apart. The difference is behavioral---how the answer is \textit{constructed}. This section shows the construction difference on three questions, each worked at all three levels. The levels here follow the platform-company convention: L5 $\approx$ senior, L6 $\approx$ staff, L7 $\approx$ senior staff/principal; labs use different labels but calibrate the same behaviors.

\subsection{Worked Pair 1: ``How Would You Improve Search Relevance?''}

An applied-design classic (the full technical treatment is Volume~III's territory, [SR~4--5, SR~7]; here we care only about answer construction).

\textbf{The L5 answer.}
\begin{quote}\itshape
``There's a lot we could do. Upgrade the embedding model for retrieval and mine hard negatives; add a cross-encoder reranker over the top 100; improve query understanding---spell correction, expansion, intent classification; use click data to train a learning-to-rank model over hand-crafted plus semantic features; maybe fine-tune on domain data. Hybrid lexical-plus-vector retrieval usually beats either alone, so I'd do that too.''
\end{quote}

\textbf{Why this is L5.} Every sentence is correct, and the list is respectably complete---that is exactly the problem. It is a catalog: techniques enumerated without priority, cost, expected magnitude, or any question asked of the interviewer. Nothing distinguishes this candidate from the documentation of a search library. The tell of an L5 answer is that it could be given without knowing anything about \textit{this} search system.

\textbf{The L6 answer.}
\begin{quote}\itshape
``First I need to know where relevance is actually lost, because the fix depends entirely on that. What's the metric---NDCG@10 on a judged set, or online engagement? And how do losses split by segment: head versus tail queries, navigational versus exploratory? Say we find the head is fine and tail queries fail at retrieval---then no reranker will save us, because the right documents never reach it; recall@100 tells us that in an afternoon. If tail retrieval is the problem, the sequence is: hybrid retrieval first---cheap, low-risk, typically a large tail win; then a reranker over a bigger candidate set---weeks of work, needs a latency budget, but reliably worth several NDCG points when retrieval is healthy; then embedding fine-tuning on our click data with hard negatives, which is the most powerful and most operationally expensive step, so it goes last and only if the first two leave a measurable gap. Each step ships behind an interleaving experiment so we know its individual contribution.''
\end{quote}

\textbf{Why this is L6.} Three construction moves, none of them about knowing more. First, \textbf{diagnosis before treatment}: the answer starts by locating the loss (metric, segment, retrieval-vs-ranking split) rather than treating ``relevance'' as one thing. Second, \textbf{sequencing with decision criteria}: the same techniques appear as in the L5 answer, but ordered by cost and risk, each with a named condition for doing it at all (``only if the first two leave a measurable gap''). Third, \textbf{quantification and measurement}: recall@100 as the discriminating cheap experiment, interleaving to attribute credit. The interviewer can now delegate this person a quarter of work.

\textbf{The L7 answer.}
\begin{quote}\itshape
``Before optimizing relevance I want to know why we believe relevance is the binding constraint. If the judged-set NDCG is high and users still churn, the problem may be coverage, freshness, or that our judgments don't reflect what users mean by good---and improving NDCG will improve nothing. So my first deliverable is the measurement program, not a model: do our offline judgments predict online preference? If interleaving wins don't track NDCG gains, the eval is broken and every team optimizing against it is wasted spend. Second, where's the leverage: if three product teams each run their own bespoke rerankers, the highest-impact move may be a shared retrieval platform with a standing eval so improvements compound instead of forking. Then the technical sequence looks like [the L6 plan], executed by the team---my job is to make sure that a year from now the eval still discriminates, the wins persisted, and we can tell which of the five shipped changes did what.''
\end{quote}

\textbf{Why this is L7.} The candidate \textbf{questions the metric} before optimizing it, and treats the evaluation program itself as the first deliverable. They \textbf{scope the organizational problem}---who else is spending effort here, whether the work compounds---and they plan for the system of people and measurement that outlives the quarter. Note what L7 is \textit{not}: it is not skipping the technical content (the L6 plan is explicitly embedded), and it is not vague strategy talk. The L7 answer contains the L6 answer, plus a challenge to its premise, plus a leverage argument.

\subsection{Worked Pair 2: ``Inference Costs Must Drop 40\% This Quarter Without Hurting Quality''}

The levers themselves are Chapter~\ref{chap:inference_optimization}'s content; this pair is about deployment of that knowledge.

\textbf{The L5 answer.}
\begin{quote}\itshape
``Quantize to INT8 or FP8---that's usually close to free quality-wise. Add continuous batching if we don't have it, and prefix caching for shared prompts. Distill the model into a smaller one for easy traffic. Speculative decoding speeds up generation. Together that should get to 40\%.''
\end{quote}

\textbf{Why this is L5.} Correct levers, no cost model. ``Together that should get to 40\%'' is hope, not arithmetic. There is no ordering, no risk assessment, and---the giveaway---no definition of what ``not hurting quality'' will mean operationally.

\textbf{The L6 answer.}
\begin{quote}\itshape
``Step one is a cost decomposition, because the levers attack different terms: what fraction of spend is which model, and within the big one, prefill versus decode, and what's our GPU utilization at peak versus trough? Three findings change everything: if utilization is 30\%, better batching and autoscaling might deliver half the target with zero model risk---that's first. If prefill dominates because of long shared system prompts, prefix caching is a large, low-risk win. Then quantization: FP8 serving is typically a 30--40\% cost reduction with quality changes small enough to gate with our standard eval suite---but `gate' is the operative word, so before touching anything I need the quality bar defined: which evals, what regression threshold, who signs off. Distillation and model-tier routing are the biggest levers and the riskiest---a router sending 40\% of traffic to a cheaper model can hit the target alone, but misrouting damage is user-visible, so it ships last, behind an A/B with guardrail metrics. Sequence: measurement, utilization, caching, quantization, routing---each with an expected percentage and a kill criterion, and I'd expect to hit 40\% without needing the riskiest step at full scale.''
\end{quote}

\textbf{Why this is L6.} \textbf{Quantification first}: the answer refuses to pick levers before decomposing where the money goes. \textbf{Sequencing by risk-adjusted return}, with an expected magnitude per step and an explicit quality-gating mechanism. \textbf{Failure-mode fluency}: misrouting damage, regression thresholds, kill criteria. The 40\% target is treated as an arithmetic obligation, not a vibe.

\textbf{The L7 answer.}
\begin{quote}\itshape
``Two questions before the plan. First, is 40\% of serving cost the right target, or a proxy for `unit economics are broken'? If cost per conversation is the real problem, changing the traffic mix---shorter contexts, capping agentic retries, product changes to reduce wasted generations---may be cheaper than any systems work, and it's owned by a different team, which means the real deliverable is a shared cost-per-request dashboard that makes the tradeoff visible to both orgs. Second, what does the quarter deadline do to reversibility? Distillation locks in a quality ceiling that takes months to walk back; I'd spend the quarter's political capital making the safe levers land fast rather than rushing the irreversible one. Then the execution is [the L6 plan]---and the piece I'd personally own is the quality-gating and attribution infrastructure, because a cost program without a regression story is how you hit the number in March and spend Q3 debugging `the model got worse.' The dashboard and the gates are what make the 40\% still be true in a year.''
\end{quote}

\textbf{Why this is L7.} The target itself is interrogated (cost per what?), the org boundary is named as part of the problem, reversibility enters the sequencing logic, and the candidate chooses their personal leverage point (the measurement and gating infrastructure) rather than claiming all the work. Again: the L6 plan is contained, not replaced.

\subsection{Worked Pair 3: ``How Do You Know Your New Model Is Actually Better?''}

Eval mechanics are Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation_metrics} (benchmark depth in Volume~II [NLP~10]); this pair is about the answer's shape.

\textbf{The L5 answer.}
\begin{quote}\itshape
``Run the benchmark suite and compare scores, check a held-out eval set for our task, and then A/B test it in production against the current model on our main engagement metric.''
\end{quote}

\textbf{Why this is L5.} The three-word version of a real answer. No error bars, no contamination concern, no notion that the benchmarks and the product metric can disagree, no statement of what decision the evaluation is feeding.

\textbf{The L6 answer.}
\begin{quote}\itshape
``Three tiers, each catching what the previous one can't. Tier one: automated evals as a CI gate---capability suites plus our task-specific sets, with confidence intervals, because a 1-point move on a 500-question benchmark is noise; and a contamination check, since a `better' score you trained on is worse than no score. Tier two: LLM-as-judge pairwise comparisons on live-traffic-shaped prompts, with the standard bias controls---position swapping, rubric-anchored scoring, periodic human calibration of the judge. Tier three: human preference on a stratified sample, then a production A/B with the primary metric plus guardrails---latency, refusal rate, safety flags---because offline wins routinely fail to transfer, and the guardrails catch the regressions the headline metric hides. `Better' is then a decision rule stated in advance: ship if tier one shows no regression beyond threshold, tier two wins significantly, and the A/B guardrails hold.''
\end{quote}

\textbf{Why this is L6.} A structure (tiers) with a reason for each tier, statistical hygiene, named failure modes of each measurement (contamination, judge bias, offline-online gap), and---the staff tell---a pre-stated decision rule rather than ``look at the numbers and decide.''

\textbf{The L7 answer.}
\begin{quote}\itshape
``First: better for which decision? An eval that gates weekly model pushes, an eval that steers training, and an eval that convinces enterprise customers are three different instruments, and conflating them is how eval programs fail. Assuming ship-gating: [the L6 tiers], plus the two problems that kill eval programs over time. One, saturation---our discriminating evals stop discriminating as models improve, so the program needs a standing process for retiring saturated sets and sourcing hard cases from production failures; eval refresh is a permanent workstream, not a project. Two, Goodhart---the moment tier one gates ship decisions, teams optimize against it, so the held-out portions rotate and the judge gets audited against fresh human data on a schedule. And organizationally: the eval program needs an owner whose incentives are calibration, not shipping---if the team that wants to launch also owns the bar, the bar moves. I'd rather have a slightly worse eval owned independently than a perfect one owned by the launch team.''
\end{quote}

\textbf{Why this is L7.} The metric's \textit{purpose} is interrogated first; the program is designed against its own decay (saturation, Goodhart); and the organizational failure mode---who owns the bar---is treated as a first-class design constraint. This is what ``plans the eval program'' means as an L7 signal.

\subsection{The Generalizable Deltas}

\begin{keyinsight}[What Actually Separates the Levels]
Five construction moves recur across every question type; interviewers score them whether or not the rubric names them.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Quantification first.} L6+ answers put arithmetic before architecture: decompose the cost, size the workload, estimate the win per lever. A number volunteered unprompted is worth ten adjectives.
    \item \textbf{Constraint discovery before solutioning.} L6+ answers begin by asking what the metric is, where the loss concentrates, what the latency/cost/quality bar is. The L5 tell is an answer that would be identical for any company.
    \item \textbf{Failure-mode fluency.} L6+ answers attach to every proposal the way it breaks, the way you'd detect it, and the kill criterion. ``What breaks'' fluency is the single most reliable depth signal.
    \item \textbf{Decision criteria over catalogs.} L5 lists options; L6 sequences them with the condition under which each is chosen. The deliverable of an L6 answer is a plan someone could execute without you.
    \item \textbf{Org and leverage framing (L7).} L7 answers question the objective, locate the work in the organization (who else is spending here, what compounds, who owns the bar), choose a personal leverage point, and build the measurement that keeps the win true after they stop watching. L7 contains L6; it never skips it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{keyinsight}

\begin{warningbox}
Two calibration traps. First, \textbf{committees level on your weakest consistent signal}, not your best round: a brilliant depth round plus senior-scope behavioral stories yields ``hire at L5''---down-leveling, not rejection, is the most common bad outcome for strong candidates, and it is decided mostly in the design, deep-dive, and behavioral rounds. Second, \textbf{L7 framing without L6 substance reads as evasion}: questioning the metric and then failing the follow-up arithmetic is worse than a clean L6 answer. Earn the altitude---demonstrate the plan, then challenge its premise, in that order if you are unsure what the interviewer wants.
\end{warningbox}

\section{The Project Deep Dive}
\label{sec:project_deep_dive}
%==============================================================================

The deep dive is 60 minutes on one or two projects from your history, taken apart forensically by a senior engineer. It is the loop's authenticity check: does the resume survive contact? It rarely produces the top positive signal by itself, but it produces vetoes constantly, and at staff level the committee reads this round's writeup first. More otherwise-strong staff candidates are rejected here than in any technical round---almost always on ownership, not on technical content.

\subsection{The Narrative Structure}

A deep-dive narrative has five beats. Interviewers do not need you to label them, but they notice when one is missing.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Problem.} What was broken or missing, for whom, and why it mattered then. One or two sentences; resist backstory.
    \item \textbf{Scale and constraints.} The numbers that make the problem hard: traffic, data size, model size, latency budget, team size, deadline. This beat is what makes every later decision legible---a choice that looks wrong in the abstract is usually right under the real constraints, and stating them first is what lets the interviewer follow your judgment instead of second-guessing it.
    \item \textbf{The two or three decisions that mattered}---each with the alternatives you rejected and why. This is the heart. Not a chronology of everything that happened: the specific forks where a different competent engineer might have gone the other way, the evidence you used, and what you gave up. ``We chose X'' is a fact; ``we chose X over Y because the data showed Z, at the cost of W'' is a staff signal.
    \item \textbf{Measured impact.} Before/after on the metric that mattered, and---critically---the decomposition: where each point of the gain came from, and which parts were yours versus the team's. Round numbers that cannot be decomposed read as embellishment.
    \item \textbf{Retrospective honesty.} What you got wrong, what you would do differently now, and what the project changed about how you work. A narrative with no failures reads as either shallow involvement or dishonesty; volunteered negative results are among the strongest trust signals in the whole loop.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{A Worked 90-Second Opening}

The opening sets the interviewer's map for the whole hour. It should cover beats 1--2, headline beats 3--4, and stop---inviting the drill rather than pre-empting it:

\begin{quote}\itshape
``The project I'll use is our retrieval-and-reranking stack rebuild. Context: 40M queries a day against 200M documents; relevance complaints were our top support category, and p95 latency was 900\,ms against a 400\,ms product requirement---so we had a quality problem and a latency problem, and the existing system, lexical retrieval plus a GBDT ranker, couldn't fix either. Team of five; I was the tech lead and wrote the retrieval side myself. Three decisions shaped it: hybrid retrieval instead of pure vector---our tail queries are heavy on part numbers and exact codes, which embeddings mangle; a cross-encoder reranker distilled to a 30\,ms budget instead of serving the teacher, which is where most of the latency risk lived; and killing my own first design---a unified embedding for retrieval and ranking---after three weeks, when offline results showed it losing to the two-model setup on tail queries. Outcome: NDCG@10 up 11\%, about 7 points from retrieval and 4 from reranking by ablation; p95 to 340\,ms; support complaints halved in a quarter. The thing I'd do differently is the migration plan---I under-invested in dual-running the old stack and we ate two weeks of incident pain I'd have paid one week of engineering to avoid. Where do you want to go deep?''
\end{quote}

Every sentence is doing work: constraints with numbers, decisions with rejected alternatives and reasons, impact decomposed by ablation, ownership stated plainly (``wrote the retrieval side myself''), a failure volunteered, and an explicit handoff. Ninety seconds, then yield---interviewers score what survives their probing, not what you managed to say uninterrupted.

\subsection{How Interviewers Probe}

\textbf{Drilling the ``we.''} Expect ownership decomposition, asked more bluntly than candidates expect: ``Whose work was this really? Draw the team and point at your box.'' ``What would have happened if you weren't there?'' The staff bar is having been the \textit{decider} on the decisions you narrate, not the adjacent observer. Answer in ``I'' for your parts and give credit crisply for the rest---inflated ownership is detected by decomposition, and one detection poisons the whole loop.

\textbf{Pressure-testing the numbers.} ``You said 40\% latency improvement---decompose it. Where did each piece come from? What did you try that didn't work?'' Real owners decompose fluently, know which gains were measured versus estimated, and remember the dead ends. Prepare the decomposition of every number on your resume to one level of depth; if you cannot decompose it, take it off the resume.

\textbf{``What would you do differently?''} This is not a politeness question; it is scored. A real answer names a specific decision, the information you missed or misweighed, and the changed process you now use. ``Nothing major---it went pretty well'' is a scored miss.

Prepare two to three projects total, each as a one-page brief: context, constraints, the decision tree with rejected alternatives and why, metric decomposition, failures, aftermath, and your specific fingerprints. This is the highest-leverage prep hour in the loop.

\begin{warningbox}
Deep-dive red flags that reject staff candidates: \textbf{impact without mechanism} (``we improved conversion 20\%'' with no account of what changed and why it worked); \textbf{no failed attempts} (a frictionless narrative signals shallow involvement); \textbf{blaming others} (the worst decision on the project was always someone else's); \textbf{``we'' that never resolves to ``I''} under direct questioning; \textbf{round numbers that fall apart} when decomposed; and \textbf{choosing the impressive project over the owned one}. All six are avoidable with preparation, which is exactly why committees weight this round: it measures honesty and self-knowledge at leisure, not performance under pressure.
\end{warningbox}

\section{ML System Design at Staff Level}
\label{sec:staff_system_design}
%==============================================================================

This section deliberately teaches no design content. The frameworks canon is Chapter~\ref{chap:decision_frameworks}; component depth lives in Chapters~\ref{chap:inference_optimization}, \ref{chap:production_systems}, \ref{chap:pretraining_at_scale}, and \ref{chap:gpu_fundamentals}; and the design questions distributed across all four volumes---serving and training designs here, RAG and production NLP in Volume~II [NLP~9, NLP~12], retrieval and ranking systems in Volume~III [SR~1--8], experimentation design in Volume~IV [CML~6]---are the drill set. What belongs in this chapter is the meta-skill: the procedure that staff candidates impose on any design prompt, which is what the round actually grades.

\subsection{The Five Moves}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Constraint discovery.} Interrogate the prompt before designing: users and traffic, latency and cost budgets, quality bar and error asymmetry, what exists already, what ``done'' means. Two to three minutes of questions---and state aloud which constraint you believe is the expensive one. Solutioneering before pinning requirements is the most common opening failure.
    \item \textbf{Quantify early.} Do the workload math before drawing boxes: QPS, tokens, bytes, GPUs, dollars. The arithmetic is the entry fee---``and then we shard it,'' with no numbers, is the archetypal staff-level reject in this round. Build a personal numbers card (hardware specs, memory formulas, KV-cache math, cost anchors) and drill it until the arithmetic is instant.
    \item \textbf{Propose, then iterate.} Get a complete, working v1 on the board in ten to fifteen minutes---end to end, every component named---then harden it in layers where the interviewer steers. A finished simple design beats an unfinished sophisticated one in every rubric. Say explicitly what you are consciously deferring.
    \item \textbf{Failure modes as first-class.} For each component: how it fails, how you detect it, what degrades gracefully. Training-side designs live or die on checkpoint/recovery and straggler stories; serving-side on overload behavior and regression detection. Volunteering these unprompted is a strong-hire marker.
    \item \textbf{The eval plan, always.} Every design ends with how you know it works: the metrics, the gates, the experiment that validates the riskiest assumption first. At frontier labs, ``how do you know it works'' asked twice without a crisp answer is a reject.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keyinsight}[Mid-Design Mutations Are the Point]
Interviewers change constraints mid-design deliberately---``latency budget just dropped 10$\times$,'' ``now it's multi-region,'' ``your GPU allocation was halved.'' This is not sabotage; it is the test. The graded behavior is returning to move 2: re-run the arithmetic under the new constraint, identify which components survive, and renegotiate the requirements you now cannot meet. Candidates who defend the old design, and candidates who silently start over, both fail the same probe. Say: ``that changes the binding constraint from memory to bandwidth---three things break, here is what I'd cut.''
\end{keyinsight}

One boundary worth naming: when the prompt is a \textit{product} problem (``add memory to our assistant''), design the measurement loop and the dumbest v1 that validates demand, then earn complexity. When the prompt is an \textit{infrastructure} problem (``pretraining on 16k GPUs''), design for failure as the normal case. Research-track candidates asked for a system should not design the model; product-track candidates should not disdain the product framing---both are recurring, fatal category errors.

\subsection{Answering Without Sounding Canned}

STAR (situation, task, action, result) is a fine \textit{skeleton} and a terrible \textit{script}. Interviewers trained to probe read a polished STAR monologue as evasive; the texture they are checking for lives in three ingredients the script omits:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Specifics.} Names of tradeoffs, numbers, artifacts, what you actually said in the room. True stories have detail three levels deep; invented or borrowed ones collapse on the second probe.
    \item \textbf{Your reasoning at the decision point.} Not just what you did but what you weighed, what you almost did instead, and what information would have flipped you. This is where the technical-judgment vocabulary of Chapter~\ref{chap:decision_frameworks} pays off in a nominally non-technical round.
    \item \textbf{The cost you paid.} Real staff stories have prices: political capital spent, a relationship strained and repaired, a quarter of your own roadmap sacrificed, being wrong in public. A story where leadership was free is a story the interviewer discounts.
\end{enumerate}

Preparation is a story matrix, not memorized answers: eight to ten true stories against the scenario axes above, each rehearsed aloud at two-minute length, each selected for \textit{maximal honest scope}---multi-team, quarter-plus-horizon stories where they truthfully exist. Scope mismatch (good stories that are all single-team, single-feature sized) down-levels more staff candidates than any technical round; if your best stories are senior-sized, tell them at full honesty and let the loop calibrate, but check first whether you are underselling scope you actually had.

\subsection{Values Fit at Frontier Labs}
\label{sec:values_fit}

Anthropic runs an explicit mission conversation; OpenAI probes mission alignment inside HM and behavioral rounds; both are real conversations with real reject rates. Three things to understand about them:

\textbf{They calibrate thoughtfulness, not doctrine.} Informed disagreement, argued well, scores fine---often better than fluent agreement. What fails is recited enthusiasm without depth: one ``which specific argument moved you?'' exposes it, and it grades worse than honest agnosticism.

\textbf{Prep the thinking, not a script.} Read the lab's actual published positions---for Anthropic, the responsible-scaling policy and core safety writeups; for any lab, their stated mission tensions---and form at least one genuine, defensible point of engagement or disagreement. Have true stories where integrity cost you something, because the live test is whether your stated values survive an incentive conflict: ``you're two days from launch and an eval shows a small regression in a harm category---what do you actually do?'' is answered by your reasoning process, not your conclusion (see the drill in Section~\ref{sec:staff_loop_interview}).

\textbf{Answer honestly, including uncertainty.} Saying ``I don't know, and here is how I think about the uncertainty'' is explicitly positive signal in these rounds---labs that calibrate on epistemic honesty grade hedged truth above confident performance. Doomer or accelerationist maximalism delivered without humility fails in both directions.

\section{Research Taste}
\label{sec:research_taste}
%==============================================================================

Research-taste rounds---paper discussions, ``what would you work on,'' research talks---grade a portfolio of defended opinions and the reasoning behind problem selection. A knowledge book cannot supply the opinions; it can supply the method.

\subsection{The Paper-Discussion Round}

You are asked to present or critique a paper (sometimes yours, sometimes handed to you cold). The craft is a fixed decomposition:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Contribution vs.\ claim.} State what the paper \textit{demonstrates} separately from what it \textit{claims}. The gap between the two is where every good critique lives: a method shown to work on three benchmarks at 7B scale \textit{claims} a general principle; is the evidence load-bearing for the claim, or only for the demonstration?
    \item \textbf{Ablation quality.} Were the baselines tuned as hard as the method? Compute-matched? How many seeds, and are the error bars seed-variance or a single run? Which ablation is \textit{missing}---the one whose absence is most convenient for the headline result? Weak-baseline overclaiming is the single fastest way to fail a lab research interview when the paper is your own.
    \item \textbf{What you would test next.} Propose the follow-up experiment that most efficiently discriminates the paper's explanation from the leading alternative: the hypothesis, what result would falsify it, the minimal compute to know. ``What would change your mind, and how cheaply can you find out'' is the taste signal, and it is scored more heavily than fluency in the paper's details.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{``What Would You Work On, and Why?''}

The structure interviewers reward: a \textbf{specific problem} (not an area), the \textbf{reason it is the important one} (what it unblocks, argued from mechanism), \textbf{why the field's mainline approach won't get there} (or where it will stall), and \textbf{what evidence would flip your ranking}. ``Everything is promising'' fails; so does contrarianism without receipts. The strongest answers carry visible update history---``I believed X until [result]; here is what changed and why''---because taste is calibration over time, not a static portfolio of takes.

\textbf{Taste signals interviewers name in debriefs:} problem-selection reasoning (why \textit{this}, why \textit{now}, why \textit{you}); accurate awareness of what is known versus genuinely open (misclassifying a solved problem as open, or vice versa, is heavily penalized); mechanism-level opinions rather than paper name-dropping; and the honesty to bound claims---``this matters if assumption A holds, and A is the thing I'd test first.'' Build two or three such opinions in your area and pressure-test them with smart friends until they are load-bearing; one mechanism-level opinion beats twenty skimmed abstracts.

\section{Execution Mechanics}

\subsection{Day-of Habits}

\textbf{Whiteboard and doc hygiene.} Structure the artifact before filling it: headers for requirements, workload math, design, open questions---then fill in. Keep a running requirements list in a corner and check proposals against it visibly; keep your arithmetic on the board, not in your head, so the interviewer can audit and credit it. In coding rounds: function skeletons and test cases before bodies; run the code whenever an interpreter exists.

\textbf{Thinking aloud.} Narrate decisions, not keystrokes: ``I'm choosing a dict here because we need O(1) lookup under the follow-up they'll probably add'' is signal; play-by-play of typing is noise. Silence is the failure mode---perfect silent work scores below imperfect narrated work in every rubric, because the interviewer can only grade what they can observe. When stuck, rent a hint well: state what you know, what you've ruled out, and the specific fork you want input on. Asking well is scored positively; flailing silently for 20 minutes is not.

\subsection{Recovering from a Miss Mid-Loop}

Rounds are scored independently, and interviewers generally do not compare notes until the debrief; one weak round is recoverable if it is a calibrator rather than a gate. The unforced error is contamination: carrying round $N$'s misery into round $N{+}1$, where a fresh interviewer starts you at zero. Use the break to reset physically---water, walk, two minutes of notes on what the next round needs---and drop the post-mortem until the loop ends. If you genuinely misunderstood a question's intent and realized late, saying so to the recruiter afterward occasionally helps and never hurt anyone; re-litigating it in the next round always reads badly.

\subsection{Your Questions Are Data}

The questions you ask are graded as level signal, formally or not. Staff-reading questions are about constraints and decision-making: ``what's the team's scarcest resource---compute, people, or decision bandwidth?'', ``what does the last project you killed tell me about how you make that call?'', ``where does this team's roadmap most depend on another team executing?'' Junior-reading questions are about process and perks. Prepare two real questions per interviewer, tuned to their role---and actually use the answers in your team-match decision, which is what makes them real.

\subsection{Virtual Loops}

Nearly all 2026 loops are virtual. The mechanics worth a rehearsal hour: test the actual tools (CoderPad-class editors, Excalidraw-class whiteboards) at speed beforehand, since fumbling the canvas taxes your design round's clock; camera at eye level, notes off-screen and used sparingly---interviewers notice reading; close everything else and silence notifications; state explicitly when you are pausing to think so silence is not dead air; and ask the AI-assistant rules out loud at the start of every coding round---policies now differ per company \textit{and per round}, some rounds grade how well you direct and verify an assistant, and guessing wrong in either direction is expensive.

Two honest caveats. And at labs, the flat title means the \textit{band} is the negotiation object: ask the recruiter directly where the offer sits in the band and what evidence would move it.

\subsection{Negotiation Basics}

\textbf{The main lever is a competing offer.} Real, comparable, in-hand numbers move offers; almost nothing else reliably does. This is the strongest argument for running loops in parallel rather than sequentially, and for keeping every process warm until one closes. Never bluff a number---recruiters compare notes across a small industry, and a called bluff ends the negotiation.

\textbf{What is flexible and what is not.} Base salary bands are narrow and mostly fixed per level. Equity and sign-on are where flexibility lives; level itself moves only with new evidence (a competing offer at the higher band, or loop feedback that was borderline). At labs, understand the equity instrument before comparing numbers: private-company options and profit-participation-style units differ from public-company RSUs in liquidity, taxation, and risk, and a nominally larger lab package can be worth less---or far more---than a platform offer. Ask about liquidity mechanics (tender offers, expected windows) explicitly; it is a normal question.

\textbf{Process hygiene.} Do not negotiate before the offer exists; get final numbers in writing; deadlines are usually extensible by asking, and ``I need two weeks to finish another process'' is routine. Enthusiasm and negotiation are not in tension---the strongest position is ``I want to work here, and I need the numbers to make sense.''

\subsection{Team Match at Labs}

Passing the loop at a lab typically lands you in a matching pool: multiple team conversations, then a mutual choice. Treat this as a second loop that you are also running: talk to several teams even if the first is appealing (comparison is information, and expressed demand also strengthens your negotiation); choose on manager quality, problem importance, and rate of learning, in roughly that order---at a frontier lab the problem will change under you within a year, and the manager and learning rate are what persist. Team match is also where scope gets re-litigated: the charter you match into determines whether your band's expectations are meetable, so ask each team the staff-signal questions from Section~\ref{sec:execution_mechanics} and take the answers seriously. A strong loop packet plus a weak team match is a common way to have a bad first year at a great company.

\section{Interview Questions}
\label{sec:staff_loop_interview}
%==============================================================================

The questions below are scenario drills for the rounds this chapter covers. Unlike the rest of this volume, the ``strong answer'' here is a construction pattern to instantiate with your own history---rehearse these aloud, with a skeptical partner where possible.

%--- Q1: Most impactful project ---

\begin{interviewq}
\textbf{Q: Walk me through your most impactful project. (Then 40 minutes of drilling.)}

\badgeLsix{L6} \quad \badgetype{Behavioral} \quad \badgefreq{\freqhigh}

\stronganswer

Open with the five-beat narrative of Section~\ref{sec:project_deep_dive}, delivered in 90 seconds and then yielded---see the worked opening in that section for the full pattern. The construction rules: state the constraints with numbers before any solution appears; narrate two or three \textit{decisions} with the alternatives you rejected and the evidence that killed them, not a chronology; decompose your impact number by mechanism (``11\% NDCG, roughly 7 from retrieval and 4 from reranking, by ablation'') and separate your contribution from the team's before being asked; volunteer one thing you got wrong; and end by handing the interviewer the choice of where to drill.

Then survive the drill. The three probes that decide the round: ownership decomposition (``draw the team, point at your box''---answer in ``I'' for your parts, credit others crisply), number pressure-testing (``decompose that 40\%''---know which gains were measured vs.\ estimated and what failed), and retrospective honesty (``what would you do differently''---name a specific decision, the information you misweighed, and the process you changed).

\redflags
\begin{itemize}
    \item Choosing the most impressive-sounding project over the most-owned one. A famous system you touched scores below a modest system you drove.
    \item ``We'' language that never resolves to ``I'' under direct questioning---the single most common staff-level rejection in the entire loop.
    \item Round numbers that cannot be decomposed, or impact with no mechanism.
    \item A narrative with zero failed attempts---it reads as shallow involvement or embellishment, never as excellence.
\end{itemize}

\interviewtest{Whether the resume survives forensic contact: true depth of ownership, decision quality under real constraints, numerical honesty, and scar tissue. The interviewer wants to leave able to retell your hardest decision and why you were the person who made it.}

\goodgreat
\begin{itemize}
    \item \badgeLfive{L5} Clear chronological narrative with real numbers and honest ownership of a component-scale contribution.
    \item \badgeLsix{L6} Decision-centric narrative: alternatives rejected with evidence, impact decomposed by ablation, a volunteered failure, fluent ``I''/``we'' separation, and the fifth ``why'' as crisp as the first.
    \item \badgeLseven{L7} All of L6, plus the project's org context: why this was the highest-leverage problem, what you deliberately did not do, how the win was made durable (measurement, ownership, follow-through) after you moved on, and what it changed about how you choose problems.
\end{itemize}

\followups{``Whose work was this really---draw the team.'' ``Decompose that improvement number.'' ``What was the worst technical decision on the project, and what did you do about it?'' ``What would have happened without you?''}
\end{interviewq}

%--- Q2: Peer launch hurting your metric ---

\begin{interviewq}
\textbf{Q: A peer team's launch is hurting your metric. Their leadership is celebrating it. What do you do?}

\badgeLsix{L6} \quad \badgetype{Behavioral} \quad \badgefreq{\freqhigh}

\stronganswer

\textbf{Verify before escalating.} First establish that the causal story is real: attribution analysis, holdback or before/after with the launch timeline, effect size with error bars. Arriving with ``your launch cost us 3\% on X, here's the analysis, here's the segment breakdown'' is an engineering conversation; arriving with a suspicion is a political one. Half the time this analysis reveals a shared-metric definition problem or a genuine tradeoff the org chose deliberately---in which case the right answer is different (see below).

\textbf{Peer first, escalation criteria named.} Take the analysis to the peer team's TL directly, framed as a joint problem: their launch is presumably net-positive on \textit{their} metric, so the real question is whether the org is trading correctly between the two. Propose concrete mechanisms---a guardrail metric on their future launches, a joint experiment isolating the interaction, a technical fix that recovers your loss without reverting their win. State your escalation criterion openly: ``if we can't agree on the measurement by [date], let's take it to [the shared decision owner] together''---escalation as a scheduled, transparent step, not an ambush.

\textbf{The L7 turn.} Recognize when the conflict is a symptom: two teams optimizing local metrics with no shared tradeoff currency is an org-design bug. The durable fix is a composite or guardrail structure agreed above both teams, so the \textit{next} launch doesn't recreate the fight. Proposing that---and volunteering to drive it---is the difference between resolving a conflict and removing its cause.

\redflags
\begin{itemize}
    \item Escalating before verifying, or before talking to the peer team at all.
    \item Framing it as zero-sum (``their win is illegitimate'') rather than as an org-level tradeoff to be priced.
    \item No measurement plan---pure relationship management with no analysis is not a staff answer.
    \item Bitterness or blame in the telling, if answered from experience. How you narrate the counterparty is scored.
\end{itemize}

\interviewtest{Influence without authority under a real incentive conflict: do you generate evidence, go direct, name escalation criteria transparently, and think about the org mechanism that caused the collision---or do you either avoid the conflict or weaponize it?}

\goodgreat
\begin{itemize}
    \item \badgeLfive{L5} Verifies the effect and raises it constructively with the peer team.
    \item \badgeLsix{L6} Full sequence: rigorous attribution, direct peer conversation with concrete joint mechanisms, transparent escalation criteria, and a real story from experience with the cost paid.
    \item \badgeLseven{L7} Diagnoses the missing tradeoff currency as the root cause and proposes the guardrail/composite-metric structure that prevents recurrence---plus the honest acknowledgment that sometimes the org \textit{should} accept your metric's loss, and what evidence would make you concede that.
\end{itemize}

\followups{``What if their VP refuses the guardrail?'' ``What if the analysis shows it's a genuine tradeoff and the org sides with them?'' ``Tell me about a time this actually happened to you.''}
\end{interviewq}

%--- Q3: Year and a small team ---

\begin{interviewq}
\textbf{Q: If we gave you a year and a small team, what would you work on, and why?}

\badgeLseven{L7} \quad \badgetype{Behavioral} \quad \badgefreq{\freqmed}

\stronganswer

This is a research-taste question wearing a planning costume; it is scored on problem-selection reasoning (Section~\ref{sec:research_taste}), not on the impressiveness of the topic. The construction that works:

\textbf{A specific problem, not an area.} ``Evaluation that keeps discriminating as models saturate benchmarks'' is a problem; ``evals'' is an area. Specificity is the first taste signal.

\textbf{Why this problem: argued from mechanism and leverage.} What does solving it unblock, and why is it the binding constraint rather than a nice-to-have? Why is it tractable \textit{now}---what changed (a new capability, a new data source, a cost curve crossing a threshold)? And why you: what in your track record makes your team the right owner?

\textbf{Why the mainline approach stalls.} Name the field's current default and where its returns diminish, with a mechanism---this is where ``opinionated with receipts'' is demonstrated. Steelman the default before departing from it.

\textbf{The falsifiable year plan.} Quarter one produces the result that tests the riskiest assumption---state what outcome would kill the project and what you'd do then (redirect, not persist). A year plan whose first checkpoint cannot fail is not a plan; it's a wish. End with what you'd expect to have shipped, measured, and learned at month twelve, and what evidence would flip you to the second-ranked problem on your list.

\redflags
\begin{itemize}
    \item ``Everything in this space is promising''---survey answers fail; the question demands a ranking with reasons.
    \item Contrarianism without receipts: rejecting the mainline approach with no mechanism-level argument.
    \item A plan with no kill criterion, or one whose first falsifiable result arrives in month eleven.
    \item Picking a problem with no connection to your demonstrated strengths---taste includes knowing your own comparative advantage.
\end{itemize}

\interviewtest{Research taste as calibration: specific, mechanism-argued, falsifiable problem selection with visible awareness of what is known vs.\ open---and whether you plan like an owner (riskiest assumption first) or like a grant application.}

\goodgreat
\begin{itemize}
    \item \badgeLfive{L5} Names a reasonable problem and a sensible plan of attack.
    \item \badgeLsix{L6} Specific problem with a mechanism-level argument for why it matters and why now, plus a quarter-one experiment that could fail.
    \item \badgeLseven{L7} All of L6, plus honest ranking against the alternatives (``I'd work on A over B because\ldots; the result that would flip me is\ldots''), a steelman of the mainline approach, and an account of why their team specifically---ending with what the org should do with the result in either outcome.
\end{itemize}

\followups{``What result in the first quarter would make you kill it?'' ``Why isn't [the obvious mainline approach] going to get there first?'' ``Rank that against the two next-best problems.''}
\end{interviewq}

%--- Q4: Critique this paper's evaluation ---

\begin{interviewq}
\textbf{Q: Here's a paper claiming a new post-training method beats RLHF. Critique its evaluation.}

\badgeLsix{L6} \quad \badgetype{Behavioral} \quad \badgefreq{\freqmed}

\stronganswer

Run the three-part decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:research_taste}, in order:

\textbf{Contribution vs.\ claim.} What was demonstrated (method X outperforms baseline Y on benchmarks Z at scale S) versus what is claimed (``beats RLHF'')? The gap is the critique: does the evidence generalize across scales, data regimes, and task families, or is the claim an extrapolation from one operating point?

\textbf{Baseline strength.} The decisive question for any ``beats RLHF'' paper: was the RLHF baseline tuned as hard as the method? Same compute, same data budget, hyperparameters searched with equal effort? Under-tuned baselines are the most common defect in comparison papers, and interviewers deliberately choose papers with this flaw. Adjacent checks: compute-matched comparisons (does the method's gain survive when the baseline gets its extra FLOPs?), seed variance (are error bars across seeds, or is this a single run?), and eval hygiene (judge-model bias if LLM-judged, contamination between training and eval data, benchmark saturation making deltas noise).

\textbf{The missing ablation and the next experiment.} Name the ablation whose absence most flatters the result---often the component the authors are most attached to. Then propose the follow-up that discriminates the paper's explanation from the leading alternative: the hypothesis, the result that would falsify it, and the minimal compute to know. Ending with a cheap, decisive experiment is the taste signal this round exists to find.

\redflags
\begin{itemize}
    \item Nitpicking presentation (figure quality, missing citations) instead of evidence structure.
    \item Pure negativity: a critique with no account of what the paper \textit{does} establish reads as taste-signaling, not taste.
    \item Accepting the headline comparison without asking whether the baseline was tuned---the canonical planted flaw.
    \item No proposed follow-up experiment, or one that costs a frontier-scale training run to learn anything.
\end{itemize}

\interviewtest{Whether you evaluate evidence the way a reviewer for your own team's research would: claim-evidence gap analysis, baseline fairness, ablation quality, and the instinct to propose the cheapest discriminating experiment rather than a vague ``more experiments needed.''}

\goodgreat
\begin{itemize}
    \item \badgeLfive{L5} Checks benchmarks, baselines, and statistical basics; spots obvious weaknesses.
    \item \badgeLsix{L6} Separates contribution from claim, interrogates baseline tuning and compute matching, names the missing ablation, and proposes a concrete falsifying follow-up.
    \item \badgeLseven{L7} All of L6, plus placement: what the field would actually learn if the claim held, which prior results it would tension with, and a calibrated bottom line (``I'd bet the effect is real at this scale but the mechanism is [alternative]; here's the cheapest test'').
\end{itemize}

\followups{``The authors respond that tuning the baseline harder is unfair---respond.'' ``You have one week of cluster time to test this paper---what do you run?'' ``Would you adopt this method for our pipeline today?''}
\end{interviewq}

%--- Q5: Harm regression before launch ---

\begin{interviewq}
\textbf{Q: You're two days from a launch your team has crunched for, and an eval shows a small but real regression in a harm category. Walk me through what you actually do.}

\badgeLsix{L6} \quad \badgetype{Behavioral} \quad \badgefreq{\freqmed}

\stronganswer

This is the live test of whether your stated values survive an incentive conflict (Section~\ref{sec:values_fit}). The graded content is your reasoning process; a memorized ``safety first'' conclusion without process scores poorly.

\textbf{First, make the signal real.} Within hours, not days: is the regression statistically solid (sample size, error bars, rerun with fresh seeds) and is the harness comparing like with like (same prompts, templates, judge)? A real regression and an eval artifact demand different actions, and confusing them in either direction is costly.

\textbf{Then, size and characterize it.} Which harm category, what severity, what exposure---a small regression on a high-severity, high-traffic category is not ``small.'' Check whether it is localized (a fixable behavior, a filterable pattern) or diffuse. This determines whether a two-day mitigation exists: a targeted system-prompt or filter patch, gating the affected capability, or a partial launch that excludes the affected surface.

\textbf{Decide with the framework the org already owns---visibly.} If the org has a launch policy with harm thresholds, this is exactly what it is for: present the finding, the mitigation options with costs, and a recommendation to the launch owner---escalation here is process working, not failure. If no such policy exists, make the call transparently (my default: slip the launch before shipping a known, uncharacterized harm regression---launch dates are recoverable; shipped harm and the credibility of your eval program are much less so) and then \textbf{fix the process}: this regression surfaced two days out because harm evals ran too late in the cycle, and the durable deliverable is moving them into CI so the next team is never in this position.

\redflags
\begin{itemize}
    \item Deciding before verifying---either shipping on ``it's probably noise'' or slipping the launch without checking the harness.
    \item Treating it as a solo hero decision when a launch-review process exists to price exactly this tradeoff.
    \item Pure cost-free virtue: an answer that never acknowledges what slipping costs the team reads as untested.
    \item Framing safety and product as enemies rather than as a tradeoff the org must price deliberately.
\end{itemize}

\interviewtest{Epistemic honesty and integrity under pressure: verify-then-size-then-decide discipline, willingness to escalate through real processes, honesty about the costs, and whether you convert the incident into a process fix. At Anthropic-style values rounds this question, or a sibling of it, is close to guaranteed.}

\goodgreat
\begin{itemize}
    \item \badgeLfive{L5} Verifies the result, weighs the tradeoff honestly, and would delay if the regression is real.
    \item \badgeLsix{L6} Full process: rapid verification incl.\ harness forensics, severity-times-exposure sizing, concrete two-day mitigation options, transparent use of the org's launch governance, and honest accounting of the cost to the team.
    \item \badgeLseven{L7} All of L6, plus the systemic view: why the eval fired this late, the CI-stage fix that prevents recurrence, and a true story of having paid this kind of cost before---told with the political price included.
\end{itemize}

\followups{``The launch owner overrules you and ships---what do you do?'' ``What if the regression is in a gray-area category your team disagrees about?'' ``Tell me about a time you pushed back on shipping and it cost you.''}
\end{interviewq}

%--- Q6: Disagree and commit ---

\begin{interviewq}
\textbf{Q: Describe committing to a technical decision you argued against. Would you do it again?}

\badgeLsix{L6} \quad \badgetype{Behavioral} \quad \badgefreq{\freqhigh}

\stronganswer

The construction has four parts, and interviewers probe for whichever you omit:

\textbf{The disagreement, with technical specifics.} What you argued, to whom, with what evidence---demonstrating that your objection was substantive (a real risk, quantified where possible), argued in the room, and lost on the merits or on legitimate strategic grounds, not ignored.

\textbf{The commit, made real.} What committing actually looked like: you staffed the chosen path properly, defended it to your own skeptical reports, and did not run a shadow effort or narrate ``told you so'' in hallways. The test of real commitment is that an outsider could not tell from your execution which side you had argued.

\textbf{The honest outcome.} Either direction works. If you turned out right, what mattered is \textit{how} the org found out---ideally via the checkpoint or metric you negotiated at commit time (``I'll commit fully, and let's agree on what evidence would reopen this'')---and that you reopened the decision with data, not blame. If you turned out wrong, say what your objection misweighed and what it taught your judgment. Both tellings need the cost you paid.

\textbf{``Would you do it again?''} The strong answer is a calibrated yes-with-conditions: yes, because teams that relitigate every decision ship nothing, and the commit bought execution speed worth more than my objection's expected value---but with the boundary named: decisions that are irreversible, or that cross safety or integrity lines, don't get disagree-and-commit; they get escalation. Knowing which decisions are two-way doors is the staff-level content of the answer.

\redflags
\begin{itemize}
    \item A story where you were vindicated and the telling is a victory lap---it signals you never actually committed.
    \item No mechanism for reopening the decision: commitment without a feedback loop is just obedience.
    \item ``I'd do it again, always''---the unconditional version misses that some decisions must not be committed to.
    \item Disparaging the decision-makers, or a story with no real stakes.
\end{itemize}

\interviewtest{Whether you can lose an argument and still multiply the team: substantive disagreement, genuine commitment, evidence-based reopening, and a calibrated model of when disagree-and-commit is the wrong tool.}

\goodgreat
\begin{itemize}
    \item \badgeLfive{L5} A true story with real disagreement, real commitment, and an honest outcome.
    \item \badgeLsix{L6} Adds the negotiated checkpoint at commit time, visible-commitment specifics (what you told your own team), and the two-way-door boundary in the ``again?'' answer.
    \item \badgeLseven{L7} All of L6, told at multi-team scope, plus what the episode changed about how you \textit{run} decisions for others---e.g., you now institutionalize the reopening criteria so your teams can commit fast without losing the feedback loop.
\end{itemize}

\followups{``What evidence did you agree would reopen it?'' ``How did you handle your reports who agreed with you?'' ``Give me the version where you were wrong.''}
\end{interviewq}

%--- Q7: First month, no direction ---

\begin{interviewq}
\textbf{Q: If you joined and we gave you no direction for your first month, what would you do---concretely, in week one?}

\badgeLseven{L7} \quad \badgetype{Behavioral} \quad \badgefreq{\freqmed}

\stronganswer

An agency probe (OpenAI-flavored, but asked everywhere): it is scored on concreteness and initiative, and ``I'd schedule meetings to align on priorities'' is the canonical wrong answer. The winning shape is a specific, artifact-producing week one that shows you generate direction rather than wait for it:

\textbf{Days 1--2: touch the real system.} Get the dev environment running, trace one request or one training step end to end, read the last month of incidents/postmortems and the current top-priority docs. Ship something tiny but real---a broken test fixed, a doc corrected, a flaky alert silenced---because the fastest way to learn a system's texture is to change it.

\textbf{Days 2--4: find the pain empirically.} Read the dashboards and eval reports before asking anyone what matters, then talk to five people---the TL, two engineers on the critical path, the PM or research lead, one heavy internal user---asking each the same two questions: what's the most expensive thing this team does by hand, and what does everyone know is broken but nobody owns?

\textbf{Days 4--5: produce the artifact.} A one-page memo: here are the three highest-leverage problems I found, here is the evidence, here is the one I propose to take, here is what I'll have shipped or measured in two weeks and how we'll know it worked. Send it to the TL and manager \textit{as a proposal you begin executing}, not as a request for permission---explicitly inviting redirection: ``tell me where this is wrong; otherwise this is what I'm doing.''

The meta-signal: week one ends with the team having \textit{more} direction than before you arrived, which is the actual job description of staff-plus in a high-autonomy org.

\redflags
\begin{itemize}
    \item Process reflexes: meetings, alignment, roadmap-reading as the primary activity---the classic reject for big-company candidates at labs.
    \item Grand refactors or architecture opinions before touching the system.
    \item A week one with no shipped artifact and no falsifiable two-week plan.
    \item Pure heads-down coding with no evidence-gathering---agency without judgment is the other failure mode.
\end{itemize}

\interviewtest{High-agency behavior under ambiguity: do you generate direction from evidence, produce artifacts on day-level timelines, and self-correct via a proposal loop---or do you need structure handed to you?}

\goodgreat
\begin{itemize}
    \item \badgeLfive{L5} Sensible onboarding: environment up, code read, people met, small fix shipped.
    \item \badgeLsix{L6} The full arc: empirical pain-finding, a written prioritized proposal with a two-week falsifiable plan, and execution begun without waiting.
    \item \badgeLseven{L7} All of L6, tuned to the specific team's context (names the kind of leverage problem \textit{this} team likely has), with the explicit theory that the deliverable is direction---and a track record example of having done exactly this.
\end{itemize}

\followups{``What if your week-one memo's top problem is something leadership disagrees is a problem?'' ``What did you actually do in your first month at your last job?'' ``How does this change if the team is mid-crisis when you land?''}
\end{interviewq}

%--- Q8: First ten minutes of an ambiguous design prompt ---

\begin{interviewq}
\textbf{Q: I'm going to give you a deliberately vague prompt---``design memory for our assistant.'' Run the first ten minutes.}

\badgeLsix{L6} \quad \badgetype{System Design} \quad \badgefreq{\freqhigh}

\stronganswer

This drills the opening moves of Section~\ref{sec:staff_system_design}; every design round begins with some version of it, stated or not. The first ten minutes, on the clock:

\textbf{Minutes 0--3: constraint discovery, out loud.} Who is this for and what does memory \textit{do} for them---personalization, task continuity, long-horizon projects? What's the scale (users, conversations, tokens of history per user)? Latency and cost budget per request? Privacy constraints---user-visible, user-editable, deletable-on-demand? What exists today? Then the staff move: name the expensive requirement---``I think the tension is quality-of-recall versus privacy-and-cost; if memory retrieval adds 300\,ms and a privacy surface to every request, that dominates the design---agreed?'' You are negotiating the problem, not collecting specs.

\textbf{Minutes 3--5: quantify the workload.} Rough arithmetic on the board: users $\times$ history growth $\times$ bytes; per-request retrieval budget; what fits in context versus what must be retrieved; cost per user-month at the proposed design point. The numbers immediately prune the design space---e.g., ``at this scale, full-history-in-context dies at month two; so this is a write-time distillation plus retrieval problem.''

\textbf{Minutes 5--10: propose the complete v1, end to end.} Write path (what gets extracted and stored, when), storage and index, read path (retrieval into context, budget), the update/decay story, the privacy surface, and---stated in the same breath---the eval: ``how we know memory helps: a task-continuity eval suite plus an online guardrail that it never hurts response quality for users with no useful memory.'' Close the ten minutes by listing what you deferred and asking where to go deep.

The graded content is the \textit{procedure}: constraints interrogated before solutioning, arithmetic before boxes, a complete v1 fast, eval attached, deferrals explicit. The domain content it draws on lives in Chapter~\ref{chap:decision_frameworks} and the per-volume design chapters.

\redflags
\begin{itemize}
    \item Solutioneering: an architecture appears in minute one, requirements never do.
    \item Twenty questions with no proposal---interrogation must convert into a design by minute ten.
    \item No arithmetic anywhere in the opening: box-drawing without numbers is the archetypal staff design reject.
    \item No eval story, or privacy/deletion treated as an afterthought when the prompt is user data.
\end{itemize}

\interviewtest{Whether you impose the staff design procedure on an ambiguous prompt: constraint discovery with a named expensive requirement, early quantification that prunes the space, a complete fast v1, and measurement as part of the design rather than an appendix.}

\goodgreat
\begin{itemize}
    \item \badgeLfive{L5} Asks sensible clarifying questions and produces a reasonable architecture.
    \item \badgeLsix{L6} Runs the full procedure on the clock: names the binding constraint, does workload arithmetic that visibly prunes options, lands a complete v1 with an eval plan by minute ten, and lists deferrals.
    \item \badgeLseven{L7} All of L6, plus product judgment: questions whether ``memory'' is the right feature framing for the user problem, proposes the dumbest v1 that validates it (e.g., explicit user-pinned facts before automatic extraction), and sketches the failure-and-iteration story---what will be wrong in v1 and how the system tells you.
\end{itemize}

\followups{``Your retrieval budget just dropped to 50\,ms---what changes?'' ``A user asks what you remember about them and demands deletion---walk me through the path.'' ``How do you keep memory from making the assistant worse for brand-new users?''}
\end{interviewq}
